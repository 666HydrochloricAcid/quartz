# 9.0 Introduction
---
So far, we have studied sets equipped with a single binary operation. Yet the integers, the motivating example of group theory, carry far more structure than $(\mathbb{Z},+)$ reveals. We can also multiply integers, and most of what makes arithmetic interesting, such as divisibility, primes, and factorization, concerns multiplication and its interaction with addition. If groups are the abstraction of symmetry, rings are the abstraction of arithmetic.

A ring is a set equipped with two binary operations, each well behaved on its own: addition makes it an abelian group, and multiplication is associative and has an identity. The only axiom linking the two operations is the distributive law. As we shall see, familiar facts such as $0\cdot a=0$ and $(-a)b=-(ab)$ are not separate axioms but consequences of distributivity alone.

Much of what follows will look familiar, since most notions of group theory have direct analogues for rings :

<div align='center'>

|            Groups            |        Rings         |
| :--------------------------: | :------------------: |
|           Subgroup           |       Subring        |
|       Normal subgroup        |        Ideal         |
|      Factor group $G/N$      |  Factor ring $R/I$   |
| Kernels are normal subgroups |  Kernels are ideals  |

</div>

The only genuinely new ingredient is the **ideal**. One might expect subrings to play the role of normal subgroups, but they cannot. A ring homomorphism $f:R\to S$ will be required to satisfy $f(1_R)=1_S$, so $1_R\in\ker f$ only when $1_S=0_S$, that is, only when $S$ is the zero ring. Hence the kernel of a ring homomorphism is almost never a subring. Ideals are precisely the subsets that occur as kernels, and they are exactly what we need to form factor rings.

The name comes from number theory. Every integer $n\ge2$ factors uniquely into primes, by the [[Mathematics/Number Theory/index|Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic]], and it is tempting to assume that the same holds in larger number systems. In 1847, _Lamé_ presented a proof of _Fermat's Last Theorem_ that rested on exactly this assumption for certain rings of cyclotomic integers. The assumption fails. Already in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]=\set{a+b\sqrt{-5}:a,b\in\mathbb{Z}}$, we have

$$
6=2\cdot3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5}),
$$

and none of the four factors can be factored further in any nontrivial way. _Kummer_, who had already observed such failures, recovered a form of unique factorization by introducing what he called **ideal numbers**. _Dedekind_ later recast these as the ideals we study today.

This history raises a question that will guide the second half of our study of rings. The ring $\mathbb{Z}$ and the ring $F[x]$ of polynomials over a field $F$ share a remarkable list of properties. Each has a [[1. Divisibility|division algorithm]], and hence greatest common divisors and a Euclidean algorithm, and in each, factorization into irreducible elements is unique. Why should two such different rings behave so much alike, and why does $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ not? We shall answer this by isolating three abstract properties that $\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[x]$ both enjoy: having a division algorithm, having every ideal generated by a single element, and having unique factorization. Each of these properties will turn out to imply the next.
# 9.1 Rings
---
From now on, we are going to think about some structures equipped with **two** binary operations.

<div class='def'>

**Definition 9.1.1 \[Rings\]** A set $R$ is called a **ring** if it has two binary operations, written as addition and multiplication, satisfying for all $a$, $b$, and $c$ in $R$ :

&nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; $(\text{R}1)$&emsp;$a+b=b+a$,

&nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; $(\text{R}2)$&emsp;$a+(b+c)=(a+b)+c$,

&nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; $(\text{R}3)$&emsp;There exists $0\in R$ such that $0+a=a$ for all $a\in R$,

&nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; $(\text{R}4)$&emsp;For each $a\in R$, there exists $-a\in R$ such that $a+(-a)=0$,

&nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; $(\text{R}5)$&emsp;$a(bc)=(ab)c$,

&nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; $(\text{R}6)$&emsp;There exists $1\in R$ such that $1a=a1=a$ for all $a\in R$,

&nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; $(\text{R}7)$&emsp;$a(b+c)=ab+ac$ and $(b+c)a=ba+ca$.

And $R$ is called a **commutative ring** if, in addition,

&nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; &nbsp; $(\text{R}8)$&emsp;$ab=ba$.

</div>

The first four axioms gives that a ring $R$ is an additive abelian group. The additive unity 0 in $(\text{R}3)$ is called the **zero** of $R$, and the additive inverse $-a$ of $a$ in $(\text{R}4)$ is called the **negative** of the element $a$. $(\text{R}5)$ and $(\text{R}6)$ gives that $R$ is a multiplicative monoid, so the element 1, called the **unity** of $R$, is unique. If the ring must be emphasized, we write the zero and unity as $0_R$ and $1_R$. Each identity in $(\text{R}7)$ is called the the **(left and right) distributive law**, and this connects addition and multiplication.

Some authors call any structure satisfying all of the axioms except possibly $(\text{R}6)$ a ring, but we shall instead call such a structure a **general ring**.

In addition, when the operations, zero, or unity of a ring $R$ are not the usual ones, or when we want to emphasize them, we explicitly write that $(R,+_R,\cdot_R,0_R,1_R)$ is a ring. Otherwise, we simply say that $R$ is a ring and write $+$, $\cdot$, $0$, and $1$ without subscripts.

<div class='ex'>

**Example 9.1.2** The following is rings :
1. The set $R=\set{0}$ is a ring where $0+0=0$ and $0\cdot0=0$. It is called the **zero  ring** and denoted $R=0$.
2. Each of $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, and $\mathbb{C}$ is a commutative ring.
3. $\mathbb{Z}_n$ is a commutative ring for each $n\ge2$.
4. $M_n(\mathbb{R})$ equipped with matrix addition and multiplication is a ring, where $0_{M_n(\mathbb{R})}=0_n$ and $1_{M_n(\mathbb{R})}=I_n$. It is not commutative if $n\ge2$.
5. $\mathbb{R}[x]$ equipped with usual addition and multiplication is a commutative ring.
6. If $X$ is a nonempty set, then the set $F(X,\mathbb{R})$ of all functions $X\to\mathbb{R}$, equipped with pointwise addition and multiplication is a commutative ring, where $0_{F(X,\mathbb{R})}$ is the zero function and $1_{F(X,\mathbb{R})}$ is the constant function 1.
7. If $R_1$, $R_2$, $\cdots$, $R_n$ be rings, then the Cartesian product $R=R_1\times R_2\times\cdots\times R_n$ equipped with componentwise addition and multiplication is a ring, where $0_R=(0_{R_1},0_{R_2},\cdots,0_{R_n})$ and $1_R=(1_{R_1},1_{R_2},\cdots,1_{R_n})$. Moreover, it is commutative if and only if each $R_i$ is commutative.

Now, we shall observe some properties for rings.

Since a ring $R$ is an additive abelian group, the laws of exponents take on a different form : For $n\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $a\in R$, we write the $n^{\text{th}}$ power $a^n$ of a as the $n^{\text{th}}$ multiple $na$ of $a$ in an additive group.

$$
\begin{align}
&0a=0 &&a^0=0 \\ &1a=a &&a^1=a \\ &(-1)a=-a &&a^{(-1)}=a^{-1} \\ &(n+m)a=na+ma &&a^{n+m}=a^na^m \\ &n(a+b)=na+nb &&(ab)^n=a^nb^n\quad\text{(if }ab=ba\text{)} \\ &n(ma)=(nm)a &&(a^m)^n=a^{mn}
\end{align}
$$

If $1_R$ denotes the unity  of $R$, we write $1_R+1_R=2$, $1_R+1_R+1_R=3$, and so on. More generally, we write

$$
k\cdot1_R=k,\quad\text{for all integers }k
$$

when no confusion can result. This notation is consistent with our convention of writing $\mathbb{Z}_n=\set{0,1,\cdots,n-1}$. We are interested in how this multiplication by integers relates to the multiplication in $R$.

<div class='thm'>

**Theorem 9.1.3** For all $r\in R$, $0\cdot r=r\cdot0=0$. ^thm-9-1-3

</div>

<details class="proof">
<summary>Proof.</summary>

$$
\begin{align*}
0\cdot r+0\cdot r&=(0+0)\cdot r \tag{\text{R}7}\\
&=0\cdot r\tag{\text{R}3}\\
&=0\cdot r+0\tag{\text{R}3}\\
\end{align*}
$$
This gives that

$$
\begin{align*}
&(-0\cdot r)+0\cdot r+0\cdot r=(-0\cdot r)+0\cdot r+0\tag{\text{R}4}\\
\implies&(-0\cdot r+0\cdot r)+0\cdot r= (-0\cdot r+0\cdot r)+0\tag{\text{R}2}\\
\implies&0+0\cdot r=0+0\tag{\text{R}4}\\
\implies&0\cdot r=0\tag{\text{R}3}\\
\end{align*}
$$

Similarly, we can obtain that $r\cdot0=0$.

</details>

<div class='thm'>

**Theorem 9.1.4** Let $r$ and $s$ be arbitrary elements of a ring $R$. Then,

1. $(-r)s=r(-s)=-(rs)$.
2. $(-r)(-s)=rs$.
3. $(mr)(ns)=ms(rs)$ for all integers $m$ and $n$.

</div>

<details class="proof">
<summary>Proof.</summary>

By <a href="/Mathematics/Algebra/9.-Rings#^thm-9-1-3/" class="internal">Theorem 9.1.3</a>,

$$
(-r)s+rs=(-r+r)s=0s=-(rs)+rs.
$$

Hence, $(-r)s=-(rs)$ by cancellation. Similarly, $r(-s)=-(rs)$, i.e., (i) holds. And (i) gives that

$$
(-r)(-s)=r[-(-s)]=rs,
$$

i.e., (ii) holds.

In case of (iii), we first claim that

$$
r(ns)=n(rs),\qquad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{Z}.\tag{$\ast$}
$$

By <a href="/Mathematics/Algebra/9.-Rings#^thm-9-1-3/" class="internal">Theorem 9.1.3</a>, this holds for $n=0$. If if holds for some $n\ge0$, then

$$
r[(n+1)s]=r(ns+s)=r(ns)+rs=n(rs)+1(rs)=(n+1)rs.
$$

Hence, by induction, ($\ast$) holds for all $n\ge0$. If $n\lt0$, write $n=-m$, $m/gt0$. Then

$$
r(ns)=r[-(ms)]=-[r(ms)]=-[m(rs)]=n(rs),
$$

which proves ($\ast$). Similarly, $(mr)s=m(rs)$ holds for all $m\in\mathbb{Z}$. These two equations imply (iii).

</details>

If $r$ and $s$ are elements of a ring $R$, their **difference** $r-s$ is defined by

$$
r-s=r+(-s).
$$

Thus the equation $x+s=r$ in $R$ has the unique solution $x=r-s$. As for numbers, we say that $r-s$ is the result of **subtracting** $s$ from $r$.

<div class='def'>

**Definition 9.1.5 \[Characteristics\]** Let $1_R$ be the unity in a ring $R$. We define the **characteristic** of $R$, denoted $\text{char }R$, in terms of the order of $1_R$ in the additive group $(R,+)$ :

$$
\text{char }R=
\begin{cases}
n & \text{if }o(1_R)=n\text{ in the additive group }(R,+)\\
0 & \text{if }o(1_R)=\infty\text{ in the additive group }(R,+)
\end{cases}.
$$

</div>

If  $k$ is an integer, we write $kR=0$ if $kr=0$ for each $r\in R$. This happens if and only if $k1_R=0$ by [[9. Rings#^thm-9-1-3|Theorem 9.1.3]], so we obtain

<div class='thm'>

**Theorem 9.1.6** If $R$ is a ring and $\text{char }R=n$ then

1. If $\text{char }R=n\gt0$, then $kr=0$ if and only if $n\mid k$.
2. If $\text{char }R=0$, then $kR=0$ if and only if $k=0$.

</div>

<div class='ex'>

**Example 9.1.7** Each of $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, and $\mathbb{C}$ has characteristic 0. Given $n\ge2$, the  ring $\mathbb{Z}_2$ has characteristic $n$.

</div>

# 9.2 Subrings
---
<div class='def'>

**Definition 9.2.1 \[Subrings\]** If $R$ is a ring, a subset $S$ is called a **subring** of $R$ if it is a ring with the same operations (including the same unity) as $R$.

</div>

In other words, a subring $S$ of $R$ is an additive subgroup of $R$ that contains the identity of $R$ and is closed under multiplication.

<div class='thm'>

**Theorem 9.2.2 \[Subring Test\]** A subset $S$ of a ring $R$ is a subring if and only if the following conditions are satisfied :

1. $0\in S$ and $1\in S$.
2. If $s,t\in S$, then $s+t$, $st$, and $-s$ are all in $S$.

</div>

Note that the condition $0\in S$ in (i) is redundant, since we can obtain that $1-1=0\in S$. Moreover, the three closure requirements in (ii) can be reduced to two : If $s,t\in S$, then $st\in S$ and $s-t\in S$.

<div class='ex'>

**Example 9.2.3** If $i^2=-1$ in $\mathbb{C}$, write $\mathbb{Z}(i)=\{n+mi\in\mathbb{C}:m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}$. Then $\mathbb{Z}(i)$ is a subring of $\mathbb{C}$ by the subring test, called the ring of **gaussian integers**.

</div>

<div class='ex'>

**Example 9.2.4** If $R$ is any ring, let

$$
T_2(R)=\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}
a & b\\
0 & c
\end{pmatrix}
:a,b,c\in R\right\}
$$

be the ring of **upper triangular matrices** over $R$. By the subring test, $T_2(R)$ is subring of $M_2(R)$.

</div>

<div class='ex'>

**Example 9.2.5\[Centers\]** If $R$ is a ring, the **center** $Z(R)$ of $R$ is a subring of $R$, where

$$
Z(R)=\left\{z\in R:zr=rz\quad\text{for all }r\in R\right\}.
$$
Elements in $Z(R)$ are said to be **central** in $R$.

</div>

<details class="proof">
<summary>Solution.</summary>

Clearly, $1_Rr=r1_R$ for all $r\in R$.

Suppose that $z_1,z_2\in Z(R)$. Then it follows that

$$
(z_1z_2)r=z_1(z_2r)=z_1(rz_2)=(z_1r)z_2=(rz_1)z_2=r(z_1z_2),
$$

and

$$
(z_1-z_2)r=(z_1r)-(z_2r)=(rz_1)-(rz_2)=r(z_1z_2)
$$

hold for all $r\in R$.

Thus, by the subring test, the center $Z(R)$ of $R$ is a subring of $R$. 

</details>

<div class='def'>

**Definition 9.2.6 \[Idempotent\]** An element $e$ in a ring $R$ is called an **idempotent** of $e^2=e$.

</div>

<div class='thm'>

**Theorem 9.2.7 \[Corners\]** If $e$ is an idempotent in a ring $R$, write $eRe=\{ere:r\in R\}$. Then $eRe$ is a ring with unity $e$, and $eRe=\left\{a\in R:ea=a=ae\right\}$.

</div>

<details class="proof">
<summary>Proof.</summary>

Let $r,s$ be arbitrary elements in $R$. We have $0=e0e$, $ere+ese=e(r+s)e$, and $-ere=e(-r)e$. Hence, $eRe$ is an additive subgroup of $R$ that is closed under multiplication.

The definition of an idempotent gives that $e=eee\in R$ and $e(ere)=ere=(ere)e$. Thus $e$ is the unity of $eRe$, and $eRe\subseteq\left\{a\in R:ae=a=ea\right\}$.

Finally, for some $s\in\left\{a\in R:ae=a=ea\right\}$, $s=se=(es)e=ese\in eRe$, i.e., $eRe\supseteq\left\{a\in R:ae=a=ea\right\}$.

We conclude that $eRe$ is a ring with unity $e$, and $eRe=\left\{a\in R:ea=a=ae\right\}$.

</details>

The rings $eRe$, with $e^2=e\in R$, are called **corners** of the ring $R$.

# 9.3 Units and Division Rings
---
Now, we shall refer to the multiplicative inverse, the unit.

<div class='def'>

**Definition 9.3.1 \[Units\]** An element $u$ in a ring $R$ is called a **unit** if $u$ has a multiplicative inverse in $R$, denoted $u^{-1}$, so that $u(u^{-1})=(u^{-1})u=1$.

</div>

Note that the units in a ring $R$ can be **cancelled**. More precisely, if $u$ is a unit in $R$ and $r$, $s\in R$ then

$$
ur=us\implies=s\qquad\text{and}\qquad ru=su\implies=s,
$$

by the left and right multiplication by $u^{-1}$.

The set of all units in a ring $R$, denoted $R^{\ast}$, is a multiplicative group called the **group of units** of the ring $R$.

<div class='ex'>

**Example 9.3.2** Observe the following examples of units.

1. $Z(i)^{\ast}=\set{1,-1,i,-i}$
2. If $R$ is a commutative ring, then $M_n(R)^{\ast}=\{A\in M_n(R):\det A\in R^{\ast}\}$.

</div>

If a ring $R$ is not the zero ring, the zero element is never a unit, since otherwise $1=0^{-1}0=0$ by [[9. Rings#^thm-9-1-3|Theorem 9.1.3]]. Hence, every unit in $R$ must be nonzero.

<div class='def'>

**Definition 9.3.3 \[Division rings and Fields\]** A ring $R\neq0$ is called a **division ring** if every nonzero element of $R$ is a unit, that is. if $R^{\ast}=R\setminus\set{0}$. A commutative division ring is called a **field**.

</div>

<div class='ex'>

**Example 9.3.4** $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, and $\mathbb{C}$ are fields. If $p$ is a prime, $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is a field. However, $\mathbb{Z}$ is Not a field.

</div>

<div class='def'>

**Definition 9.3.5 \[Nilpotent\]** An element  $a$ in a ring $R$ is called a **nilpotent** if $a^k=0$ for some positive integer $k$.

</div>

<div class='ex'>

**Example 9.3.6**Each of the following elements is nilpotent in the given ring : 

1. $0$ is a nilpotent in every ring.
2. $2$, $4\in\mathbb{Z}_8$.
3. $\begin{pmatrix}0 & r \\0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$, $\begin{pmatrix}0 & 0 \\r & 0\end{pmatrix}\in M_2(R)$.
4. $\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 \\1 & 1\end{pmatrix}\in M_2(\mathbb{Z}_2)$.

</div>

Now we shall observe some theorems about nilpotent.

<div class='thm'>

**Theorem 9.3.7** If $a$ is a nilpotent in $R$, then $1-a$, $1+a$ are units.

</div>

<details class='proof'>
<summary>Proof.</summary>

If $a^k=0$ for some positive integer $k$, then $a^n=0$ for all $n\ge k$ by <a href='/Mathematics/Algebra/9. RIngs#^thm-9-1-3/'>Theorem 9.1.3</a>. Hence $u=1+a+a^2+\cdots$ is a finite sum, and $u\in R$. Then $(1-a)u=u(1-a)=1$. Hence $(1-a)$ is a unit. As $(-a)^k=0$, $(1+a)$ is also a unit.

</details>

<div class='thm'>

**Theorem 9.3.8** If a ring $R$ has no nonzero nilpotent elements, then all idempotents in $R$ are central.

</div>

<details class='proof'>
<summary>Proof.</summary>

If $e^2=e\in R$ and $r\in R$, set $a=er(1-e)$. Since $(1-e)e=0$, then $a^2=0$, so $a=0$ by hypothesis. It follows that $er=ere$. Similarly, $re=ere$, so $er=re=ere$.

</details>
