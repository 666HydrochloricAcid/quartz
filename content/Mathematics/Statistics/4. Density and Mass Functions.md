# 4.1 Probability Mass Functions
---
Associated with a random variable $X$ and its cdf $F_X$ is another function, which records how the probability is distributed over the values that $X$ takes. For a discrete random variable this is straightforward, since by Remark 3.2.9 each value carries a positive probability of its own.

<div class='def'>

**Definition 4.1.1 [Probability Mass Functions]** The **probability mass function**, **pmf** for short, of a discrete random variable $X$ is given by ^def-4-1-1

$$
f_X(x)=P(X=x)\qquad\text{for all }x.
$$

</div>

Note that $f_X$ is defined at every real number, and that $f_X(x)=0$ unless $x$ belongs to the countable set carrying the distribution. Unlike the cdf, which is an accumulated quantity, the pmf is a genuine point probability.

<div class='ex'>

**Example 4.1.2** Toss a fair coin repeatedly and let $X$ be the number of tosses required until the first head appears, so that $X$ is a function on the sample space $\set{\text{H},\text{T}}^{\mathbb{N}}$ of Example 3.1.2. The event $\set{X=x}$ consists of those outcomes beginning with $x-1$ tails followed by a head, and these $x$ tosses are mutually independent in the sense of Definition 2.1.7, so^ex-4-1-2

$$
f_X(x)=P(X=x)=
\begin{cases}
\left(\tfrac12\right)^{x-1}\cdot\tfrac12=2^{-x}, & x=1,2,\cdots\\
0, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
$$

These values sum to $\sum^{\infty}_{x=1}2^{-x}=1$, as they must, since the probability that no head ever appears is $\lim_{n\to\infty}2^{-n}=0$ by Theorem 1.2.8 (ii).

It is worth comparing this with Example 3.2.5, where the same sample space was read as a binary expansion. The outcomes with $X=x$ are exactly those with $\omega_1=\cdots=\omega_{x-1}=0$ and $\omega_x=1$, hence exactly those with

$$
U(\omega)\in\left[2^{-x},\,2^{-x+1}\right),
$$

an interval of length $2^{-x}$. The two computations agree, and the intervals so obtained partition $(0,1]$.

</div>

The pmf and the cdf carry the same information, and each is recovered from the other.

<div class='thm'>

**Theorem 4.1.3** Let $X$ be discrete with pmf $f_X$ and cdf $F_X$. Then for every Borel set $A$, ^thm-4-1-3

$$
P(X\in A)=\sum_{x\in A}f_X(x),\qquad\text{in particular}\qquad F_X(x)=\sum_{t\le x}f_X(t),
$$

and conversely

$$
f_X(x)=F_X(x)-F_X(x^-).
$$

</div> 

<details class="proof">
<summary>Proof.</summary>

Let $S$ be a countable set with $P(X\in S)=1$. The singletons $\set{x}$ for $x\in A\cap S$ are pairwise disjoint and their union is $A\cap S$, so $(\text{P}3)$ gives

$$
P(X\in A)=P(X\in A\cap S)=\sum_{x\in A\cap S}P(X=x)=\sum_{x\in A}f_X(x),
$$

the last equality because the omitted terms vanish. Taking $A=(-\infty,x]$ gives the second display. The final identity is Remark 3.2.9.

</details> 

<div class='ex'>

**Example 4.1.4** Let $X$ be the number of heads in three tosses of a fair coin, as in Example 3.2.4. Each of the eight outcomes has probability $\tfrac18$, and the number of outcomes with exactly $x$ heads is $\binom{3}{x}$ by Case 3 of §1.3, so

$$
f_X(x)=\binom{3}{x}\frac{1}{8},\qquad x=0,1,2,3,
$$

that is, $f_X(0)=f_X(3)=\tfrac18$ and $f_X(1)=f_X(2)=\tfrac38$. Reading off the jumps of the cdf computed in Example 3.2.4 gives the same values, as Theorem 4.1.3 requires. We may now compute probabilities by summation rather than by subtracting values of $F_X$; for instance

$$
P(1\le X\le2)=f_X(1)+f_X(2)=\tfrac34.
$$

</div>

# 4.2 Probability Density Functions
---
We must be more careful in the continuous case. If $X$ is continuous in the sense of Definition 3.2.6, then $F_X$ has no jumps, and Remark 3.2.9 gives

$$P(X=x)=F_X(x)-F_X(x^-)=0\qquad\text{for every }x.$$

The function $x\mapsto P(X=x)$ is therefore identically zero and carries no information whatsoever. Defining the continuous analogue of Definition 4.1.1 by the same formula is thus useless, and some other construction is needed.

The clue is in Theorem 4.1.3. In the discrete case the cdf is recovered from $f_X$ by summation, and the analogous operation in the continuous case is integration. We therefore reverse the order of definition: rather than defining $f_X$ pointwise and summing, we ask for a function whose integral reproduces $F_X$.

<div class='def'>

**Definition 4.2.1 [Probability Density Functions]** A random variable $X$ is called **absolutely continuous** if there exists a nonnegative Borel measurable function $f_X$ such that^def-4-2-1

$$
F_X(x)=\int^{x}_{-\infty}f_X(t)\,dt\qquad\text{for all }x,
$$

the integral being taken with respect to Lebesgue measure $\lambda$. Any such $f_X$ is called a **probability density function**, **pdf** for short, of $X$.

</div>

Two points about this definition require comment, and both are taken up in Remark 4.2.5: the function $f_X$ need not exist, so that the definition is a genuine restriction on $X$ and not merely a construction; and when it does exist it is not unique, so that one should speak of _a_ pdf rather than _the_ pdf.

<div class='ex'>

**Example 4.2.2** Let $U$ be the random variable of Example 3.2.5, obtained by tossing a fair coin infinitely often and reading the outcome as a binary expansion. There we computed $F_U(u)=u$ for $0\le u\le1$, with $F_U(u)=0$ for $u\lt0$ and $F_U(u)=1$ for $u>1$. Setting^ex-4-2-2

$$
f_U(u)=
\begin{cases}1,&0\le u\le1,\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
$$

we have for $0\le x\le1$

$$
\int^{x}_{-\infty}f_U(t)\,dt=\int^{x}_{0}1\,dt=x=F_U(x),
$$

while for $x\lt0$ both sides vanish and for $x>1$ both equal $1$. Hence $U$ is absolutely continuous with pdf $f_U$.

Observe that $f_U$ is not a probability: $f_U(u)=1$ for every $u\in[0,1]$, whereas $P(U=u)=0$ for every such $u$. The value of a pdf at a point is not the probability of that point, and indeed need not even be bounded by $1$.

</div> 

<div class='rmk'>

**Remark 4.2.3** In the continuous case we may be somewhat cavalier about the specification of interval probabilities. Since $P(X=a)=P(X=b)=0$,

$$
P(a\lt X\lt b)=P(a\lt X\le b)=P(a\le X\lt b)=P(a\le X\le b)=\int^{b}_{a}f_X(t)\,dt,
$$

the last equality by Theorem 1.2.5 (i) and Definition 4.2.1. No such latitude is available in the discrete case, where the endpoints may carry substantial probability; in Example 4.1.4, for instance, $P(1\le X\le 2)=\tfrac34$ while $P(1\lt X\lt2)=0$.

</div> 

<div class='thm'>

**Theorem 4.2.4** A random variable $X$ is absolutely continuous if and only if its cdf $F_X$ is an absolutely continuous function, that is, for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $\delta>0$ such that^thm-4-2-4

$$
\sum^{n}_{i=1}(b_i-a_i)\lt\delta\implies\sum^{n}_{i=1}\left|F_X(b_i)-F_X(a_i)\right|\lt\varepsilon
$$

for every finite collection of pairwise disjoint intervals $(a_1,b_1),\cdots,(a_n,b_n)$. In that case $F_X'=f_X$ holds $\lambda$-almost everywhere, and any two pdfs of $X$ agree $\lambda$-almost everywhere.

</div>

<details class="proof">
<summary>Proof.</summary>

The equivalence and the identity $F_X'=f_X$ a.e. constitute the fundamental theorem of Lebesgue integration, for which we refer to the Measure Theory series.

For the uniqueness assertion, suppose $f$ and $g$ are both pdfs of $X$. Then for every $x$,

$$
\int^{x}_{-\infty}(f-g)\,d\lambda=F_X(x)-F_X(x)=0,
$$

and subtracting two such identities gives $\int_{(a,b]}(f-g)\,d\lambda=0$ for every interval $(a,b]$. The collection of Borel sets $A$ with $\int_A(f-g)\,d\lambda=0$ is a $\lambda$-system containing the $\pi$-system of such intervals, so by Dynkin's theorem, as in the proof of Theorem 3.2.10, it is all of $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$. Taking $A=\set{f>g}$ and $A=\set{f\lt g}$ in turn yields $f=g$ $\lambda$-a.e.

</details> 

<div class='rmk'>

**Remark 4.2.5** Absolute continuity of $F_X$ is strictly stronger than continuity, so Definition 4.2.1 does not apply to every continuous random variable. Let $F$ be the Cantor function, extended by $0$ on $(-\infty,0)$ and by $1$ on $(1,\infty)$. It is nondecreasing, right-continuous, and has the correct limits at $\pm\infty$, so by Theorem 3.2.3 it is the cdf of some random variable $X$; and it is continuous, so $X$ is continuous in the sense of Definition 3.2.6. However, $F$ is constant on each of the intervals removed in the construction of the Cantor set $C$, and these have total length $1$, so $F'=0$ $\lambda$-a.e. Were ^rmk-4-2-5

$$
F(x)=\int^{x}_{-\infty}f(t)\,dt
$$

to hold for some $f$, Theorem 4.2.4 would give $f=F'=0$ $\lambda$-a.e. and hence $F\equiv0$, contradicting $F(1)=1$. Thus $X$ possesses no pdf. Note that this has nothing to do with the choice of integral: the argument is carried out for the Lebesgue integral, and replacing it by the Riemann integral only restricts the available $f$ further.

The second point concerns uniqueness. Altering a pdf on a set of Lebesgue measure zero changes no integral, so the defining relation determines $f_X$ at no single point; by Theorem 4.2.4 it determines exactly the $\lambda$-equivalence class. In Example 4.2.2 we could equally have taken $f_U=\mathbb{1}_{(0,1)}$, or assigned $f_U$ arbitrary values at the rationals. Consequently the set $\set{x:f_X(x)>0}$ is not well defined, and assertions about it are to be read as holding for some representative.

</div> 

<div class='rmk'>

**Remark 4.2.6 [Notation]** We adopt the widely used convention of writing the cdf with an uppercase letter and the corresponding pmf or pdf with the matching lowercase letter.

The expression "$X$ has a distribution given by $F_X$" is abbreviated to $X\sim F_X$, where the symbol $\sim$ is read "is distributed as". When a mass or density function has been specified we write $X\sim f_X$ in the same way, which is unambiguous by Theorem 4.1.3 and Definition 4.2.1, since either determines $F_X$. Finally, $X\sim Y$ abbreviates the statement that $X$ and $Y$ are identically distributed in the sense of Definition 3.2.7; by Theorem 3.2.10 this is equivalent to $F_X=F_Y$, and by Example 3.2.7 it is considerably weaker than $X=Y$.

</div>

# 4.3 Characterization and a Common Framework
---
There are only two requirements for a pmf or a pdf, and both are immediate consequences of the definitions.

<div class='thm'>

**Theorem 4.3.1** A function $f$ is a pmf of some random variable if and only if ^thm-4-3-1

 $(\text{a})$ $f(x)\ge0$ for all $x$, and

 $(\text{b})$ $f(x)=0$ outside a countable set, with $\sum_x f(x)=1$.

A function $f$ is a pdf of some random variable if and only if

 $(\text{a}')$ $f(x)\ge0$ for $\lambda$-almost every $x$, and

 $(\text{b}')$ $\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}f(t)\,dt=1$.

</div> 

<details class="proof">
<summary>Proof.</summary>

_Necessity._ If $f=f_X$ is a pmf, then $f(x)=P(X=x)\ge0$ by $(\text{P}1)$, and taking $A=\mathbb{R}$ in Theorem 4.1.3 gives $\sum_x f(x)=P(X\in\mathbb{R})=1$. If $f=f_X$ is a pdf, nonnegativity holds by Definition 4.2.1, and by Theorem 3.2.3 (i),

$$
\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}f(t)\,dt=\lim_{x\to\infty}\int^{x}_{-\infty}f(t)\,dt=\lim_{x\to\infty}F_X(x)=1.
$$

_Sufficiency._ Suppose $f$ satisfies $(\text{a}')$ and $(\text{b}')$ and define $F(x)=\int^{x}_{-\infty}f(t)\,dt$. Then $F$ is nondecreasing, since $f\ge0$ a.e.; it satisfies (i) of Theorem 3.2.3 by $(\text{b}')$ and by dominated convergence; and it is right-continuous, since $x_n\downarrow x$ gives $\mathbb{1}_{(-\infty,x_n]}f\to\mathbb{1}_{(-\infty,x]}f$ pointwise with $f$ as a dominating function. By Theorem 3.2.3 there is a random variable $X$ with $F_X=F$, and $f$ is by construction a pdf of $X$. The discrete case is identical with sums in place of integrals, using Theorem 1.2.3 to produce the random variable directly.

</details> 

<div class='rmk'>

**Remark 4.3.2** From a purely mathematical viewpoint, any nonnegative function with a finite positive integral may be turned into a pdf. If $h\ge0$ vanishes outside a Borel set $A$ and

$$
\int_{A}h(t)\,dt=K\in(0,\infty),
$$

then $f=h/K$ satisfies the conditions of Theorem 4.3.1 and is therefore the pdf of a random variable taking values in $A$. The constant $K$ is called a **normalizing constant**, and much of the labour in later chapters consists of evaluating one. The same remark applies verbatim to sums and pmfs.

</div>

The two definitions of this chapter were arrived at by different routes, and it is not yet apparent why both deserve the name "density". The following makes the analogy exact, and explains why summation in one case and integration in the other are the same operation performed against different measures.

<div class='rmk'>

**Remark 4.3.3** Let $\mu$ be a $\sigma$-finite measure on $(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))$ and say that $\mu$ **dominates** $X$, written $P_X\ll\mu$, if every $\mu$-null Borel set is also $P_X$-null. The Radon–Nikodym theorem asserts that in this case there is a nonnegative measurable $f$, unique up to $\mu$-null sets, with ^rmk-4-3-3

$$
P(X\in A)=\int_{A}f\,d\mu\qquad\text{for every }A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),
$$

and $f$ is called the **density of $X$ with respect to $\mu$**, written $dP_X/d\mu$. Both functions of this chapter are instances.

Let $X$ be discrete, carried by a countable set $S$, and let $\mu_{\#}$ be counting measure on $S$, which is $\sigma$-finite precisely because $S$ is countable. If $\mu_{\#}(A)=0$ then $A\cap S=\emptyset$, so $P(X\in A)=0$; hence $P_X\ll\mu_{\#}$. Since $\mu_{\#}(\set{x})=1$ for each $x\in S$, integration against $\mu_{\#}$ is summation, and the conclusion above is Theorem 4.1.3 with $f=f_X$ the pmf. Here the density is genuinely unique, the only $\mu_{\#}$-null subset of $S$ being empty.

Let $X$ instead be absolutely continuous and let $\mu=\lambda$. Then $P_X\ll\lambda$, and the conclusion is Definition 4.2.1 together with the uniqueness assertion of Theorem 4.2.4, with $f=f_X$ the pdf.

Thus "sum the pmf" and "integrate the pdf" are one operation, $\int\cdot\,d\mu$, performed against two different dominating measures, and the word _density_ refers in both cases to a Radon–Nikodym derivative. The choice of $\mu$ is not part of the random variable but of our description of it, which is why a pdf is determined only up to $\lambda$-null sets while a pmf is determined outright.

</div> 

<div class='rmk'>

**Remark 4.3.4 [Lebesgue Decomposition]** The possibilities encountered so far are exhaustive. By the [[Mathematics/Measure Theory/Lebesgue Decomposition Theorem|Lebesgue decomposition theorem]], every distribution on $\mathbb{R}$ admits a unique decomposition ^rmk-4-3-4

$$
P_X=\alpha P_{\mathrm{d}}+\beta P_{\mathrm{ac}}+\gamma P_{\mathrm{sc}},\qquad\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1,\quad\alpha,\beta,\gamma\ge0,
$$

into a discrete part, a part absolutely continuous with respect to $\lambda$, and a part which is continuous but carried by a set of Lebesgue measure zero. Our examples realize each case:

<div align="center">

||$\alpha$|$\beta$|$\gamma$|has a density|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|heads in three tosses, Example 4.1.4|$1$|$0$|$0$|pmf|
|first head, Example 4.1.2|$1$|$0$|$0$|pmf|
|$U$, Example 4.2.2|$0$|$1$|$0$|pdf|
|$Z$, Remark 3.2.11|$\tfrac12$|$\tfrac12$|$0$|neither|
|Cantor, Remark 4.2.5|$0$|$0$|$1$|neither|

</div>

A pmf exists exactly when $\alpha=1$ and a pdf exactly when $\beta=1$; in every other case the distribution is still perfectly well defined by its cdf, but admits no density with respect to counting measure or to $\lambda$. In the language of Remark 4.3.3, no single one of these two measures dominates every distribution. Casella and Berger dismiss such cases as pathological and assume throughout that Definition 4.2.1 applies to every continuous random variable. We shall instead say _absolutely continuous_ whenever a pdf is intended, reserving _continuous_ for the weaker condition of Definition 3.2.6.

</div>
