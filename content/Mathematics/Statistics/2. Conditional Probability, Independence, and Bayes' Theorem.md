# 2.1 Conditional Probability and Independence
---
All of the probabilities that we have dealt with thus far have been unconditional probabilities. A sample space was defined and all probabilities were calculated with respect to that sample space. In many cases, however, we are in position to update the sample space based on new information.

<div class='def'>

**Definition 2.1.1 \[Conditional Probability\]** If $A$ and $B$ are events in $S$, and $P(B)>0$, then the **conditional probability** of $A$ given $B$, $P(A\mid B)$ is^def-2-1-1

$$
P(A\mid B)=\cfrac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}
$$

</div>

Note that what happens in the conditional probability calculation is that $B$ becomes the sample space, i.e., $P(B\mid B)=1$. The intuition is that our original sample space, $S$, has been updated to $B$. All further occurrences are then calibrated with respect to their relation to $B$. In particular, note what happens to conditional probabilities of
disjoint sets. Suppose $A$ and $B$ are disjoint, so $P(A\cap B) = 0$. It then follows that $P(A\mid B) = P(B\mid A) = 0$.

<div class='thm'>

**Theorem 2.1.2 \[Bayes' Theorem\]** Let $A$, $B$ be events in $S$ with $P(B)\ne0$ Then,^thm-2-1-2

$$
P(A\mid B)=P(B\mid A)\cfrac{P(A)}{P(B)}
$$

</div>

<details class="proof">
<summary>Proof.</summary>

Rearranging [[2. Conditional Probability, Independence, and Bayes' Theorem#^def-2-1-1|Definition 2.1.1]] gives the following equality which is useful form for calculating intersection probabilities :

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(A\mid B)P(B).\tag{1}
$$

By symmetry, we can also get that :

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(B\mid A)P(A)
$$

Furthermore, we can equate the right-hand sides of the above equations to obtain

$$
P(A\mid B)=P(B\mid A)\cfrac{P(A)}{P(B)}.
$$

</details>

By generalizing, we can also get the following form of Bayes' Theorem.

<div class='thm'>

**Theorem 2.1.3 \[Generalized Bayes' Theorem\]** Let $A_1$, $A_2$, $\cdots$ be a partition of the sample space $S$, and $B$ be any event in $S$. Then, for each $i\in\mathbb{N}$,

$$
P(A_i\mid B)=\cfrac{P(B\mid A_i)P(A_i)}{P(B)}=\cfrac{P(B\mid A_i)P(A_i)}{\sum_j{P(B\mid A_j)P(A_j)}}.
$$

</div>

<details class="proof">
<summary>Proof.</summary>

It suffices to prove that $P(B)=\sum_j{P(B\mid A_j)P(A_j)}$. Since $A_1$, $A_2$, $\cdots$ is a partition of $S$, it is clear that

$$
\bigcup_i A_i=S\implies P\left(\bigcup_i A_i\right)=P(S)=1.
$$

Then, by intersecting $B$ to the both sides of the above equation, we get

$$
B\cap\left(\bigcup_i A_i\right)=\bigcup_i \left(B\cap A_i\right)=B\cap S=B,
$$

so,

$$
\sum_iP\left(B\cap A_i\right)=P(B).
$$

Then, by the [[2. Conditional Probability, Independence, and Bayes' Theorem#^def-2-1-1|Definition 2.1.1]], we finally get

$$
P(B)=\sum_i{P(B\cap A_i)}=\sum_i{P(B\mid A_i)P(A_i)}.
$$

</details>

<div class='ex'>

**Example 2.1.4** A box contains $1000$ coins. Of these, $999$ are fair, while one is two‑headed and therefore lands heads on every toss. A coin is drawn at random from the box and tossed $n$ times, and every toss results in a head. Let $B$ denote the event that the coin drawn is the two‑headed one, and $H_n$ the event that the first $n$ tosses are all heads. The description of the experiment gives

$$
P(B)=\frac{1}{1000},\qquad P(H_n\mid B)=1,\qquad P(H_n\mid B^c)=\frac{1}{2^n},
$$

the last because the tosses of a fair coin are mutually independent in the sense of Definition 2.1.7. Since $\set{B,B^c}$ is a partition of the sample space, Theorem 2.1.3 applies and yields

$$  
P(B\mid H_n)=\frac{P(H_n\mid B)P(B)}{P(H_n\mid B)P(B)+P(H_n\mid B^c)P(B^c)}  
=\frac{\frac{1}{1000}}{\frac{1}{1000}+\frac{1}{2^n}\cdot\frac{999}{1000}}  
=\frac{2^n}{2^n+999}.  
$$

Taking $n=10$, so that ten consecutive heads have been observed,

$$
P(B\mid H_{10})=\frac{1024}{2023}\approx 0.506.
$$

Ten heads in a row is an outcome a fair coin produces with probability less than one in a thousand, and yet the evidence leaves the matter very nearly undecided. The reason is that fair coins outnumber the two‑headed coin by a factor of $999$, so the rare event is nevertheless produced by the large group about as often as it is produced with certainty by the small one.

</div>

<div class='rmk'>

**Remark 2.1.5** Observe that $P(H_{10}\mid B)=1$ while $P(B\mid H_{10})\approx\tfrac12$, and that for $n=5$ the discrepancy is sharper still:

$$
P(H_5\mid B)=1,\qquad P(B\mid H_5)=\frac{32}{1031}\approx0.031.
$$

Reversing the conditioning changes the answer by a factor of more than thirty. Treating $P(A\mid B)$ as though it were $P(B\mid A)$ is a common error, sometimes called the _Fallacy of the Inverse_; the two agree only when $P(A)=P(B)$.

The dependence on $n$ is also worth recording. Writing the conclusion of Theorem 2.1.3 as a ratio against its complement, the factor $P(H_n)$ cancels and we obtain

$$  
\frac{P(B\mid H_n)}{P(B^c\mid H_n)}
=\frac{P(B)}{P(B^c)}\times\frac{P(H_n\mid B)}{P(H_n\mid B^c)}
=\frac{1}{999}\times 2^{n}.  
$$

Each additional head doubles the odds, so the evidence accumulates multiplicatively while the prior odds remain fixed, and the two balance only once $2^n$ is of the order of $999$:

|$n$|$1$|$5$|$10$|$15$|$20$|
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
|$P(B\mid H_n)$|$0.002$|$0.031$|$0.506$|$0.970$|$0.999$|

No conclusion can be drawn from the observation alone: the same event $H_{10}$ would give $P(B\mid H_{10})=1024/1033\approx0.991$ had the box contained ten coins rather than a thousand. Both the observation and the composition of the box enter the answer, and neither can be dispensed with.

</div>

<div class='def'>

**Definition 2.1.6 \[Independence\]** Two events, $A$ and $B$ are **(statistically) independent** if

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)
$$

</div>

When event $A$ and $B$ are independent, it follows from the [[2. Conditional Probability, Independence, and Bayes' Theorem#^thm-2-1-2|Bayes' Theorem]], that

$$
P(A\mid B)=\cfrac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}=\cfrac{P(A)P(B)}{P(B)}=P(A).
$$

Also,

$$
P(B\mid A)=\cfrac{P(B\cap A)}{P(A)}=\cfrac{P(A)P(B)}{P(A)}=P(B).
$$

What we want to obtain from the conditional probability $P(B\mid A)$ is the probability that an event $B$ occurs, given that another event $A$ has occurred. Intuitively speaking, $A$ and $B$ are independent if knowing that $A$ has occurred does not change the probability that $B$ occurs. Then, we can naturally think of the following.

<div class='thm'>

**Definition 2.1.7** If $A$ and $B$ are independent events, then the following pairs of events are also independent :

1. $A$ and $B^c$,
2. $A^c$ and B,
3. $A^c$ and $B^c$.

</div>

<details class="proof">
<summary>Proof.</summary>

From [[2. Conditional Probability, Independence, and Bayes' Theorem^thm-1-2-5|Theorem 1.2.5]], we get

1. 
$$
\begin{align*}
P(A\cap B^c)&=P(A)-P(A\cap B)\\
&=P(A)-P(A)P(B)\tag{\text{independence}}\\
&=P(A)\left(1-P(B)\right)\\
&=P(A)P(B^c)
\end{align*}
$$

2.
$$
\begin{align*}
P(A^c\cap B)&=P(B)-P(A\cap B)\\
&=P(B)-P(A)P(B)\tag{\text{independence}}\\
&=P(B)\left(1-P(A)\right)\\
&=P(B)P(A^c)
\end{align*}
$$

3.
$$
\begin{align*}
P(A^c\cap B^c)&=P(A^c)-P(A^c\cap B)\\
&=P(A^c)-P(A^c)P(B)\tag{\text{ii}}\\
&=P(A^c)\left(1-P(B)\right)\\
&=P(A^c)P(B^c)
\end{align*}
$$

</details>

We now extend the notion of independence to a collection of events, and close this chapter with an example illustrating it.

<div class='def'>

**Definition 2.1.8** A collection of events $A_1$, $\cdots$, $A_n$ are **mutually independent** if for any subcollection $A_{i_1}$, $\cdots$, $A_{i_k}$, we have

$$
P\left(\bigcap^k_{j=1}A_{i_j}\right)=\prod^k_{j=1}P(A_{i_j}).
$$

</div>

<div class='rmk'>

**Remark 2.1.9** The definition requires the product formula to hold for _every_ subcollection, and it is worth counting how many conditions this amounts to. A subcollection corresponds to a choice of index set $I \subseteq \{1,\ldots,n\}$, of which there are $2^n$. Those with $|I| = 1$ impose nothing, since the required identity reads $P(A_{i}) = P(A_{i})$, and the same is true of $I = \emptyset$ under the usual conventions that an empty intersection is $\Omega$ and an empty product is $1$. Discarding these, the number of substantive conditions is

$$
\sum_{k=2}^{n}\binom{n}{k} = \sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k} - \binom{n}{0} - \binom{n}{1} = 2^{n} - n - 1.
$$

</div>

<div class='ex'>

**Example 2.1.10** Toss a fair coin three times, and let $A_i$ be the event that the $i$th toss results in a head, $i = 1, 2, 3$. The sample space consists of the $8$ equally likely sequences in $\{H, T\}^3$, and $P(A_i) = \tfrac12$ for each $i$. To verify mutual independence we must check all $2^3 - 3 - 1 = 4$ subcollections of size at least two:

$$
P(A_1\cap A_2) = P(\{HHH, HHT\}) = \tfrac14 = P(A_1)P(A_2),
$$

and similarly $P(A_1\cap A_3) = P(A_2\cap A_3) = \tfrac14$, while

$$
P(A_1\cap A_2\cap A_3) = P(\{HHH\}) = \tfrac18 = P(A_1)P(A_2)P(A_3).
$$

All four conditions hold, so $A_1, A_2, A_3$ are mutually independent.

</div>

<div class='ex'>

**Example 2.1.11** Toss a fair coin twice and define

$$
A=\set{\text{the first toss is a head}},\quad B=\set{\text{the second toss is a head}},\quad C=\set{\text{the two tosses agree}}.
$$

Then $P(A)=P(B)=P(C)=\tfrac12$, and each of the three pairwise conditions holds, since

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(A\cap C)=P(B\cap C)=P(\set{\text{HH}})=\tfrac14.
$$

The three events are therefore pairwise independent. However,

$$
P(A\cap B\cap C)=P(\set{\text{HH}})=\tfrac14\neq\tfrac18=P(A)P(B)P(C),
$$

so they are not mutually independent. The failure is intuitive: any two of the three events determine the third, since knowing the outcome of both tosses tells us whether they agree. Pairwise independence is thus strictly weaker than mutual independence, and already at $n=3$, the smallest case in which the two notions can differ.

</div>
