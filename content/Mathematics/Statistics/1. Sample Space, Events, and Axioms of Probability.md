# 1.1 Set Theory
---
One of the main objectives of a statistician is to draw conclusions about a population of objects by conducting an experiment. The first step in this endeavor is to identify the possible outcomes or, in statistical terminology, the sample space.

<div class="def">

**Definition 1.1.1 [Sample Spaces]** The set, $S$, of all possible outcomes of a particular experiment is called the **sample space** for the experiment.^def-1-1-1

</div>

If the experiment consists of tossing a coin, the sample space contains two outcomes, heads and tails; thus,

$$
S=\set{\text{H},\text{T}}.
$$

If, on the other hand, the experiment is one where the observation is the reaction time to a certain stimulus, then the sample space would consist of all positive real numbers, that is,

$$
S=\left(0,\infty\right).
$$

<div class="def">

**Definition 1.1.2 [Events]** An **event** is any collection of possible outcomes of an experiment, that is, any subset of $S$.^def-1-1-2

</div>

Let $A$ be an event, a subset of $S$. We say the event $A$ occurs if the outcome of the experiment is in the set $A$. When speaking of probabilities, we generally speak of the probability of an event, rather than a set. But we may use the terms interchangeably. Hence we might think of the concept of an event equivalently as a set as below :

- Relation
	- Inclusion
	- Equality
	- Disjointedness (leads to a partition of $S$)
- Operation
	- Union
	- Intersection
	- Complementation

Also, it satisfies the [[0. Introduction to Set Theory|rules of set operations]].

# 1.2 Probabilities
---
<div class="def">

**Definition 1.2.1 [Probability Functions]** Given a sample space $S$ and an associated <a href="/Mathematics/Measure-Theory/0. Introduction and Preliminaries#^def-0-1/" class="internal">$\sigma$-algebra</a> $\mathcal{B}$, a **probability function** is a function $P:\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\left[0,1\right]$ satisfying^def-1-2-1

$(\text{P}1)$ $P(A)\ge 0$ for all $A\in\mathcal{B}$,

$(\text{P}2)$ $P(S)=1$,

$(\text{P}3)$ If $A_1,A_2,\cdots\in\mathcal{B}$ are pairwise disjoint, then $P(\cup^{\infty}_{i=1}A_i)=\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}P(A_i)$.

</div>

The three properties given in [[1. Sample Space, Events, and Axioms of Probability#^def-1-2-1|Def 1.2.1]] are usually referred to as the **Axioms of Probability**. Moreover, the function constructed by the Axioms of Probability is called **Axiomatic Probability**. This terminology is used to compare with the concept of probability in the classical sense. [[Appendix A. Axiomatic Probability|See Also]].

<div class="ex">

**Example 1.2.2** Consider the experiment of tossing a _balanced_ coin, so $S=\set{\text{H},\text{T}}$. We assume the _balanced_ coin is equally as likely to land heads up as tails up, and hence the reasonable probability function is the one that maps equal probabilities to heads and tails, that is,^ex-1-2-2

$$
P(\set{\text{H}})=P(\set{\text{T}}).
$$

Note that this does not follow from the axioms of probability. We have used a symmetry interpretation of probability to impose the requirement that heads and tails are equally probable. Since $S=\set{\text{H}}\cup\set{\text{T}}$, we have from $(\text{P}1)$, $P(\set{\text{H}}\cup\set{\text{T}})=1$. Also, $\set{\text{H}}$ and $\set{\text{T}}$ are disjoint, so

$$
P(\set{\text{H}}\cup\set{\text{T}})=P(\set{\text{H}})+P(\set{\text{T}})=1.
$$

This implies that $P(\set{\text{H}})=P(\set{\text{T}})=\frac{1}{2}$, and the function $P$ satisfies [[1. Sample Space, Events, and Axioms of Probability#^def-1-2-1|Def 1.2.1]], so $P$ is a probability function.

On the other hand, without considering _balance_, we could define $P$ as $P(\set{\text{H}})=\frac{2}{7}$ and $P(\set{\text{H}})=\frac{5}{7}$, and this also satisfies the axioms of probability, so it is also a probability function.

</div>

Instead of checking the axioms for each new probability function, like we did in [[1. Sample Space, Events, and Axioms of Probability#^ex-1-2-2|Ex 1.2.2]], we need general methods of defining probability functions satisfying the [[1. Sample Space, Events, and Axioms of Probability#^def-1-2-1|Axioms of Probability]]. The following gives a common method of defining a legitimate probability function.

<div class="thm">

**Theorem 1.2.3** Let $S=\set{s_1,s_2,\cdots,s_n}$ be a finite set, $\mathcal{B}$ be any $\sigma$-algebra of subsets of $S$, and $p_1$, $p_2$, $\cdots$, $p_n$ be nonnegative numbers that sum to 1. For any $A\in\mathcal{B}$, define $P(A)$ by

$$
P(A)=\sum_{i:s_i\in A}p_i.
$$

Then, $P$ is a probability function on $\mathcal{B}$. Moreover, this holds even if $S=\set{s_1,s_2,\cdots}$ is a countable set.

</div>

<details class="proof">
<summary>Proof.</summary>

Suppose that $S$ is finite. For any $A\in\mathcal{B}$, since $p_i\ge0$ for all i, $P(A)=\sum_{i:s_i\in A}p_i\ge0$ $(\text{P}1)$. Now,

$$
P(A)=\sum_{i:s_i\in A}p_i=\sum^{n}_{i=1}p_i=1\ (\text{P}2).
$$

Finally, let $A_1$, $\cdots$, $A_k$ denote pairwise disjoint events. Then,

$$
P\left(\bigcup^{k}_{i=1}A_i\right)=\sum_{j:s_j\in \cup^{k}_{i=1}A_i}p_j=\sum^{k}_{i=1}\sum_{j:s_j\in A_i}p_j=\sum^{k}_{i=1}P(A_i)\ (\text{P}3).
$$

The first and third equalities hold by the definition of $P(A)$. The disjointedness of the $A_i$'s ensures that the second equality is true.

 </details>

From the [[1. Sample Space, Events, and Axioms of Probability#^def-1-2-1|Axioms of Probability]], we can construct some properties of the probability function, and these properties are helpful in  the calculation of more complicated probabilities. First we construct properties with a single event.

<div class='thm'>

**Theorem 1.2.4** If $P$ is a probability function and $A$ is any set in $\mathcal{B}$, then

1. $P(\emptyset)=0$ ;
2. $P(A)\le1$ ;
3. $P(A^c)=1-P(A)$.

</div>

<details class="proof">
<summary>Proof.</summary>

1.  Let $A_1=S$ and $A_i=\emptyset$ for $i\ge2$. These sets are pairwise disjoint and $\cup^{\infty}_{i=1}A_i=S$, so by $(\text{P}3)$,

$$
P(S)=P(S)+\sum^{\infty}_{i=2}P(\emptyset).
$$

Since $P(S)=1$ is finite, $\sum^{\infty}_{i=2}P(\emptyset)=0$, and since every term is nonnegative by $(\text{P}1)$, $P(\emptyset)=0$.

As a consequence, $P$ is **finitely additive** : if $A_1,\cdots,A_k\in\mathcal{B}$ are pairwise disjoint, put $A_i=\emptyset$ for $i>k$ and apply $(\text{P}3)$ to get

$$ P\left(\bigcup^{k}_{i=1}A_i\right)=\sum^{k}_{i=1}P(A_i)+\sum^{\infty}_{i=k+1}P(\emptyset)=\sum^{k}_{i=1}P(A_i).
$$

3.  $A$ and $A^c$ are disjoint and $A\cup A^c=S$. By $(\text{P}2)$ and finite additivity,

$$
1=P(S)=P(A\cup A^c)=P(A)+P(A^c),
$$

so $P(A^c)=1-P(A)$.

2. By (iii) and $(\text{P}1)$, $P(A)=1-P(A^c)\le1$.

 </details>

Now, we construct properties with two sets.

<div class='thm'>

**Theorem 1.2.5** If $P$ is a probability function and $A$ and $B$ are any sets in $\mathcal{B}$, then^thm-1-2-5

1. $P(B\cap A^C)=P(B)-P(A\cap B)$ ;
2. $P(A\cup  B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$ ;
3. If $A\subset B$, then $P(A)\le P(B)$.

</div>

<details class="proof">
<summary>Proof.</summary>

1. We can write $B=(B\cap A)\cup(B\cap A^c)$, and the two sets on the right are disjoint since $A$ and $A^c$ are. By finite additivity,

$$
P(B)=P(B\cap A)+P(B\cap A^c),
$$

which gives (i).

2. Similarly, $A\cup B=A\cup(B\cap A^c)$, and $A$ and $B\cap A^c$ are disjoint. Hence, using (i),

$$
P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B\cap A^c)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B).
$$

3. If $A\subset B$, then $A\cap B=A$. Therefore, using (i) and $(\text{P}1)$,

$$
0\le P(B\cap A^c)=P(B)-P(A).
$$

 </details>

Finally, we construct properties with collection of sets.

<div class='thm'>

**Theorem 1.2.6** If $P$ is a probability function, then

1. $P(A)=\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}P(A\cap C_i)$ for any partition $C_1$, $C_2$, $\cdots$ ;
2. $P(\cup^{\infty}_{i=1}A_i)\le\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}P(A_i)$ for any sets $A_1$, $A_2$, $\cdots$. **(Boole's Inequality)**

</div>

<details class="proof">
<summary>Proof.</summary>

1. By the distributive law,

$$
A=A\cap S=A\cap\left(\bigcup^{\infty}_{i=1}C_i\right)=\bigcup^{\infty}_{i=1}(A\cap C_i).
$$

For $i\neq j$, $(A\cap C_i)\cap(A\cap C_j)\subset C_i\cap C_j=\emptyset$, so the sets $A\cap C_i$ are pairwise disjoint. Hence by $(\text{P}3)$,

$$
P(A)=P\left(\bigcup^{\infty}_{i=1}(A\cap C_i)\right)=\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}P(A\cap C_i).
$$

2. We first construct a disjoint collection $A_1$, $A_2$, $\cdots$ with the same union. Define

$$
A_1=A_1,\quad A_i=A_i\setminus\left(\bigcup^{i-1}_{j=1}A_j\right)=A_i\cap\left(\bigcup^{i-1}_{j=1}A_j\right)^c,\quad i=2,3,\cdots.
$$

Each $A^*_i$ belongs to $\mathcal{B}$, since a $\sigma$-algebra is closed under countable unions and complements.

- Disjointedness. Let $k\lt i$. Then $A_k\subset A_k\subset\cup^{i-1}_{j=1}A_j$, while $A_i$ does not intersect $\cup^{i-1}_{j=1}A_j$. Hence $A_i\cap A_k=\emptyset$.
- Same union. Since $A_i\subset A_i$, we have $\cup_iA_i\subset\cup_iA_i$. Conversely, if $x\in\cup_iA_i$, let $i$ be the smallest index with $x\in A_i$. Then $x\notin A_j$ for all $j\lt i$, so $x\in A^*_i$.

Therefore, by $(\text{P}3)$ and <a href="/Mathematics/Statistics/1.-Sample-Space,-Events,-and-Axioms-of-Probability#^thm-1-2-5/" class="internal">Theorem 1.2.5</a> (iii) applied to $A^*_i\subset A_i$,

$$ P\left(\bigcup^{\infty}_{i=1}A_i\right)=P\left(\bigcup^{\infty}_{i=1}A^__i\right)=\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}P(A^__i)\le\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}P(A_i).
$$

 </details>

[[1. Sample Space, Events, and Axioms of Probability#^thm-1-2-5|Theorem 1.2.5]] (ii) also gives a useful lower bound for the probability of an intersection. Since $P(A\cup B)\le1$, rearranging (ii) yields

$$
P(A\cap B)\ge P(A)+P(B)-1.
$$

This is a special case of the following inequality, which is essentially Boole's Inequality applied to complements.

<div class='thm'>

**Theorem 1.2.7 [Bonferroni's Inequality]** If $P$ is a probability function and $A_1,\cdots,A_n\in\mathcal{B}$, then

$$
P\left(\bigcap^{n}_{i=1}A_i\right)\ge\sum^{n}_{i=1}P(A_i)-(n-1).
$$

</div>

<details class="proof"> <summary>Proof.</summary>

Apply Boole's Inequality to $A^c_1,\cdots,A^c_n$ (setting $A^c_i=\emptyset$ for $i>n$) :

$$ P\left(\bigcup^{n}_{i=1}A^c_i\right)\le\sum^{n}_{i=1}P(A^c_i). $$

By De Morgan's law, $\cup^{n}_{i=1}A^c_i=\left(\cap^{n}_{i=1}A_i\right)^c$, and by [[1. Sample Space, Events, and Axioms of Probability#^thm-1-2-4|Theorem 1.2.4]] (iii), $P(A^c_i)=1-P(A_i)$. Hence

$$ 1-P\left(\bigcap^{n}_{i=1}A_i\right)\le n-\sum^{n}_{i=1}P(A_i), $$

which is the desired inequality after rearranging.

</details>

# 1.3 Counting
---
We now consider the **process of counting**. What we count here is the number of outcomes — or combinations of outcomes — that satisfy a given condition. This is used primarily to assign probabilities on finite sample spaces. 

<div class='ex'>

**Example 1.3.1** For some years, the New York state lottery worked as follows. A player chooses any six numbers from 1,2,$\cdots$,44, and the winning number is decided by selecting six of the forty-four at random. To compute the probability of winning, we first need to count how many different groups of six numbers can be chosen from forty-four.^ex-1-3-1

</div>

Counting problems often look complicated because of the many restrictions involved. The standard strategy is to break the problem into a sequence of simple tasks which are easy to count, and then combine them by known rules. The first such rule is the following.

<div class='thm'>

**Theorem 1.3.2 [Fundamental Theorem of Counting]** If a job consists of $k$ separate tasks, the $i$th of which can be done in $n_i$ ways, $i=1,\cdots,k$, then the entire job can be done in $n_1\times n_2\times\cdots\times n_k$ ways.^thm-1-3-2

</div>

<details class="proof">
<summary>Proof.</summary>

First let $k=2$. The first task can be done in $n_1$ ways, and for each of these there are $n_2$ ways to do the second task. Thus the job can be done in

$$
\underbrace{n_2+n_2+\cdots+n_2}_{n_1\text{ terms}}=n_1\times n_2
$$

ways. Now suppose the statement holds for $k$ tasks, and consider a job of $k+1$ tasks. Regard the first $k$ tasks as a single task; by the induction, it can be done in $n_1\times\cdots\times n_k$ ways. Applying the case of two tasks, the whole job can be done in $(n_1\times\cdots\times n_k)\times n_{k+1}$ ways.

</details>

In the [[1. Sample Space, Events, and Axioms of Probability#^ex-1-3-1|Example 1.3.1]], the first number can be chosen in $44$ ways and the second in $43$ ways, so there are $44 \times 43 = 1,892$ ways of choosing the first two numbers. If, however, a number may be chosen twice, the first two numbers can be chosen in $44 \times 44 = 1,936$ ways.

The distinction in the above is between counting **with replacement** and **without replacement**. There is a second important element in any counting problem: whether or not the **order** of the tasks matters. In the lottery, if the winning numbers are drawn in the order 12, 37, 35, 9, 13, 22, does a person who chose 9, 12, 13, 22, 35, 37 win? Combining both considerations, we obtain four possible methods of counting :

<div align="center">

||**Without replacement**|**With replacement**|
|:-:|:-:|:-:|
|**Ordered**|Case 1|Case 2|
|**Unordered**|Case 3|Case 4|

Before we count, we introduce a helpful notation.

</div>

<div class="def">

**Definition 1.3.3 [Factorials]** For a positive integer $n$, $n$ **factorial**, $n!$ is the product of all positive integers less than or equal to $n$. That is,^def-1-3-3

$$
n!=n\times(n-1)\times(n-2)\times\cdots\times3\times2\times1.
$$

Furthermore, we define $0!=1$.

</div>

Now, we count all possible lottery tickets in each of the four cases, and then state the result for choosing $r$ objects from $n$.

**Case 1. Ordered, without replacement.** By [[1. Sample Space, Events, and Axioms of Probability#^thm-1-3-2|Theorem 1.3.2]], the first number can be chosen in $44$ ways, the second in $43$ ways, and so on. So there are

$$
44\times43\times42\times41\times40\times39=\frac{44!}{38!}=5{,}082{,}517{,}440
$$

possible tickets. In general, there are $n(n-1)\cdots(n-r+1)=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}$ ordered arrangements.

**Case 2. Ordered, with replacement.** Each number can now be chosen in $44$ ways, since the chosen number is replaced. So there are

$$
44^6=7{,}256{,}313{,}856
$$

possible tickets. In general, there are $n^r$ arrangements.

**Case 3. Unordered, without replacement.** We already know the number of ordered tickets, so we only need to remove the redundant orderings. By [[1. Sample Space, Events, and Axioms of Probability#^thm-1-3-2|Theorem 1.3.2]] again, six distinct numbers can be arranged in $6!$ ways, so each unordered ticket corresponds to exactly $6!$ ordered tickets. Hence the number of unordered tickets is

$$
\frac{44\times43\times42\times41\times40\times39}{6\times5\times4\times3\times2\times1}=\frac{44!}{6!\times38!}=7{,}059{,}052.
$$

This form of counting is so central in statistics that it has its own notation.

<div class="def">

**Definition 1.3.4 [Binomial Coefficients]** For nonnegative integers $n$ and $r$ with $n\ge r$, we define $n$ **choose** $r$, denotes $\binom{n}{r}$,^def-1-16-st

$$
\binom{n}{r}=\frac{n!}{r!\times(n-r)!}.
$$

</div>

So the number of unordered tickets without replacement is $\binom{44}{6}$, and in general there are $\binom{n}{r}$ such arrangements. These numbers are also called **binomial coefficients**, for a reason that will become clear when we study the [[6. Common Distributions I|binomial distribution]].

**Case 4. Unordered, with replacement.** This is the most difficult case. A first guess might be $44^6/6!$, but this is incorrect : a ticket with repeated numbers corresponds to fewer than $6!$ ordered tickets, so dividing by $6!$ overcorrects.

Instead, think of the $44$ numbers as $44$ bins, and of a ticket as a placement of $6$ markers $\text{M}$ into these bins, where a bin may receive several markers :

$$
\mid\underset{1}{\mathrm{M}}\mid\underset{2}{\ }\mid\underset{3}{\mathrm{MM}}\mid \underset{4}{\mathrm{M}}\mid\underset{5}{\ }\mid\underset{\cdots}{\cdots}\mid \underset{41}{\mathrm{M}}\mid\underset{42}{\mathrm{M}}\mid\underset{43}{\ }\mid\underset{44}{\ }\mid
$$

A ticket is completely determined by the arrangement of the markers and the walls between the bins. The two outermost walls play no part, so we only need to arrange the $43$ interior walls and the $6$ markers, $49$ objects in total. Such an arrangement is determined by choosing which $6$ of the $49$ positions are markers, so the number of tickets is

$$
\binom{44+6-1}{6}=\frac{49!}{6!\times43!}=13{,}983{,}816.
$$

In general, $r$ markers and $n-1$ interior walls give $\binom{n+r-1}{r}$ arrangements.

We summarize the four cases for the number of possible arrangements of size $r$ from $n$ objects :

<div align="center">

||**Without replacement**|**With replacement**|
|:-:|:-:|:-:|
|**Ordered**|$\cfrac{n!}{(n-r)!}$|$n^r$|
|**Unordered**|$\dbinom{n}{r}$|$\dbinom{n+r-1}{r}$|

</div>

Counting techniques are especially useful when the sample space $S$ is finite and all outcomes in $S$ are **equally likely**. Suppose that $S=\set{s_1,\cdots,s_N}$ and that $P(\set{s_i})=\frac{1}{N}$ for every $s_i$. Then, by [[1. Sample Space, Events, and Axioms of Probability#^def-1-2-1|Definition 1.2.1]] with $p_i=\frac{1}{N}$, for any event $A$,

$$
P(A)=\sum_{s_i\in A}P(\set{s_i})=\sum_{s_i\in A}\cfrac{1}{N}=\cfrac{\#\text{ of elements in }A}{\#\text{ of elements in }S}=\cfrac{\left|A\right|}{\left|S\right|}.
$$
