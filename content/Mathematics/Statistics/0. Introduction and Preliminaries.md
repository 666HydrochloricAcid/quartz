# 0.0 Introduction to Statistics
---
## Two Directions of Reasoning

Consider a coin. If we know that it is _fair_, we can ask: what is the probability of seeing seven heads in ten tosses? The answer, $\binom{10}{7}/2^{10}$, follows from the model by calculation alone. This is a question of **probability**: we start from a model and deduce what data it is likely to produce.

Now reverse the question. We have tossed the coin ten times and seen seven heads. What is the chance $p$ that this coin lands heads? Is it fair? Here the data are given and the model is unknown. This is a question of **statistics**, and it runs in the opposite direction: from data back to the mechanism that produced them.

The two directions differ in kind, not only in order. Probability is deductive. Once the model is fixed, every conclusion follows with certainty, and nothing in the conclusion goes beyond what the model already contains. Statistics is inductive. It concludes something about every future toss of the coin from finitely many tosses already made, and no amount of past data logically guarantees such a conclusion. Every theorem on this blog is a deduction, yet almost nothing we believe about the world was obtained by deduction alone.

## The Problem of Induction

_David Hume_ stated the difficulty precisely in 1748. Our expectation that the future will resemble the past cannot be proved deductively, since a world in which the pattern breaks tomorrow involves no contradiction: that the sun will not rise tomorrow is as intelligible a proposition as that it will. Nor can the expectation be justified by experience, since arguing that the future has resembled the past so far, and therefore will continue to, assumes exactly the principle in question. Any justification of induction seems either to fail or to be circular.

Statistics does not overturn this observation, and this introduction will not pretend that it does. What statistics offers is more modest and more useful: it makes inductive conclusions **precise**. It states exactly what must be assumed for a conclusion to follow, and it measures how often, under those assumptions, such conclusions go wrong.

## Laplace and the Sunrise

The clearest illustration is Laplace's answer to Hume's own example. Suppose an event has occurred on each of $n$ independent occasions. Treat its probability $p$ as unknown and, having no reason to prefer one value over another, regard every $p\in[0,1]$ as equally plausible. Then the probability that the event occurs on the next occasion is

$$
P(X_{n+1}=1\mid X_1=\cdots=X_n=1)=\cfrac{\int_0^1 p^{n+1}\,dp}{\int_0^1 p^{n}\,dp}=\cfrac{1/(n+2)}{1/(n+1)}=\cfrac{n+1}{n+2}.
$$

This is the **rule of succession**. Taking recorded history to span five thousand years, Laplace concluded that one could bet 1,826,214 to 1 that the sun would rise the next day.

The number itself matters less than what the calculation reveals. The answer was obtained only after three assumptions were made: that each occasion is governed by the same unknown $p$, that the occasions are independent given $p$, and that every value of $p$ is a priori equally plausible. The inductive leap has not disappeared. It has been moved into these assumptions, where it can be seen, examined, and replaced by better ones. This, in miniature, is what statistics does.

## Two Ways to Discipline Induction

Statistics has developed two broad ways of turning this idea into a working method.

The first evaluates **procedures** rather than single conclusions. We cannot know whether a particular conclusion drawn from a particular sample is correct, but we can prove how often a given procedure would be wrong if it were applied again and again. A 95% confidence interval is not a claim that this particular interval contains the truth with probability $0.95$; it is a guarantee that the method producing it succeeds in 95% of repetitions. Fisher described statistical reasoning as _inductive inference_. Neyman, doubting that a single inference could be justified at all, recast it as _inductive behaviour_: a rule for acting whose long-run error rates are controlled.

The second treats induction as the **revision of belief**. Uncertainty about $p$ is itself described by a probability distribution, and Bayes' theorem prescribes how that distribution should change when data arrive. Laplace's calculation above is an instance of this view. De Finetti gave it a striking foundation: if our beliefs about an infinite sequence of tosses do not change when the order of the tosses is permuted, a symmetry called **exchangeability**, then those beliefs necessarily take the form

$$
P(X_1=x_1,\cdots,X_n=x_n)=\int_0^1 p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}\,d\mu(p),\qquad k=\sum^{n}_{i=1}x_i,
$$

for some probability distribution $\mu$ on $[0,1]$. We then reason _as if_ there were an unknown $p$ with the tosses independent given $p$, not because we have assumed that the world is uniform, but because our beliefs are symmetric. The uniformity of nature reappears as a property of belief rather than as a claim about the world.

The two views disagree about what probability is, and that disagreement will resurface throughout this series, most visibly when maximum likelihood estimation and Bayesian estimation are compared.

## Where the Leap Happens

The intuition that a large sample tells us about the population becomes a theorem of probability theory. If $X_1,X_2,\cdots$ are independent and identically distributed with $\text{E}|X_1|<\infty$, the **law of large numbers** states that the sample mean converges almost surely to $\text{E}[X_1]$. Observed averages settle on the underlying expectation.

It is worth being exact about what this theorem does and does not say. Its conclusion is certain, but only because it is a deduction from its hypothesis. Whether real observations are independent and identically distributed is not something the theorem can tell us. Accepting that hypothesis for actual data is precisely the inductive step. If the hypothesis is false, because the coin is worn, the population shifts, or the measurements interfere with one another, the guarantee is lost with it. Statistics cannot eliminate this risk. It can only make the risk explicit and, in later chapters, give us tools to check it.
