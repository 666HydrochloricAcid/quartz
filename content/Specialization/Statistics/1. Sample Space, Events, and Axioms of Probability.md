One of the main objectives of a statistician is to draw conclusions about a population of objects by conducting an experiment. The first step in this endeavor is to identify the possible outcomes or, in statistical terminology, the sample space.

<div class="def">

**Definition 1.1 [Sample Spaces]** The set, $S$, of all possible outcomes of a particular experiment is called the **sample space** for the experiment.^def-1-1-st

</div>

If the experiment consists of tossing a coin, the sample space contatins six outcomes, heads and tails; thus,

$$
S=\set{\text{H},\text{T}}.
$$

If, on the other hand, the experiment where the observation is reaction time to a certain stimulus. Then, the sample space would consist of all positive real numbers, that is,

$$
S=\left(0,\infty\right).
$$

<div class="def">

**Definition 1.2 [Events]** An **event** is any collection of possible outcomes of an experiment, that is, any subset of $S$.^def-1-2-st

</div>

Let $A$ be an event, a subset of $S$. We say the event $A$ occers if the outcome of experiment is in the set $A$. When speaking of probabilities, we generally speak of the probability of an event, rather than a set. But we may use the terms interchangeably. Hence we might think of the concept of an event equivalently as a set as below :

- Relation
	- Inclusion
	- Equality
	- Disjointedness (leads to a partition of $S$)
- Operation
	- Union
	- Intersection
	- Complementation

Also, it satisfies the [[0. Introduction to Set Theory|rules of set operations]].

<div class="def">

**Definition 1.3 [Probability Functions]** Given a sample space $S$ and an associated <a href="/Specialization/Mathematics/Measure-Theory/0. Introduction and Preliminaries#^def-0-1-mt/" class="internal">$\sigma$-algebra</a> $\mathcal{B}$, a **probability function** is a function $P:\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\left[0,1\right]$ satisfying^def-1-3-st

$(\text{P}1)$ $P(A)\ge 0$ for all $A\in\mathcal{B}$,

$(\text{P}2)$ $P(S)=1$,

$(\text{P}3)$ If $A_1,A_2,\cdots\in\mathcal{B}$ are pairwise disjoint, then $P(\cup^{\infty}_{i=1}A_i)=\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}P(A_i)$.

</div>

The three properties given in [[1. Sample Space, Events, and Axioms of Probability#^def-1-3-st|Def 1.3]] are usually referred as the **Axioms of Probability**. Moreover, (the function constructed by Axioms of Probability is called **Axiomatic Probability**. This terminology is used to compare with the concept of classical sense of probability. [[content/Specialization/Machine Learning/Appendix A. Axiomatic Probability|See Also]].

**Example 1.4** Consider the experiment of tossing a _balanced_ coin, so $S=\set{\text{H},\text{T}}$. We assume the _balanced_ coin is equally as likely to land heads up as tails up, and hence the reasonable probability function is the one that maps equal probabilities to heads and tails, that is,

$$
P(\set{\text{H}})=P(\set{\text{T}}).
$$

Note that only this does not follow the axioms of probability. We have used a symmetry interpretation of probability to impose the requirement that heads and taild equally probable. Since $S=\set{\text{H}}\cup\set{\text{T}}$, we have from $(\text{P}1)$, $P(\set{\text{H}}\cup\set{\text{T}})=1$. Also, $\set{\text{H}}$ and $\set{\text{T}}$ are disjoint, so

$$
P(\set{\text{H}}\cup\set{\text{T}})=P(\set{\text{H}})+P(\set{\text{T}})=1.
$$

This implies that $P(\set{\text{H}})=P(\set{\text{T}})=\frac{1}{2}$, and the function $P$ satisfies the [[1. Sample Space, Events, and Axioms of Probability#^def-1-3-st|Def 1.3]], so $P$ is a probability function.

On the other hand, without considering _balance_, we could define $P$ as $P(\set{\text{H}})=\frac{2}{7}$ and $P(\set{\text{H}})=\frac{5}{7}$, and this also satisfies the axiom of probability, so is also a probability function.

**Theorem 1.5** Let $S=\set{s_1,s_2,\cdots,s_n}$ be a finite set, $\mathcal{B}$ be any $\sigma$-algebra of subsets of $S$, and $p_1$, $p_2$, $\cdots$, $p_n$ be nonnegative numbers that sum to 1. For any $A\in\mathcal{B}$, define $P(A)$ by

$$
P(A)=\sum_{\{i:s_i\in A\}}p_i.
$$

Then, $P$ is a probability function on $\mathcal{B}$. Moreover, this holds even if $S=\set{s_1,s_2,\cdots}$ is a countable set.

<details class="proof">
<summary>Proof.</summary>

Suppose that $S$ is finite. For any $A\in\mathcal{B}$, since $p_i\le0$ for all i, $P(A)=\sum_{i:s_i\in A}p_i\le0$ $(\text{P}1)$. Now,

$$
P(A)=\sum_{\{i:s_i\in A\}}p_i=\sum^{n}_{i=1}p_i=1\ (\text{P}2).
$$

Finally, let $A_1$, $\cdots$, $A_k$ denote pairwise disjoint events. Then,

$$
P\left(\bigcup^{k}_{i=1}A_i\right)=\sum_{\{j:s_j\in \cup^{k}_{i=1}A_i\}}p_j=\sum^{k}_{i=1}\sum_{\{j:s_j\in A_i\}}p_j=\sum^{k}_{i=1}P(A_i)\ (\text{P}3).
$$

The first and third equalities hold by the definition of $P(A)$. The disjointedness of the $A_i$'s ensures that the second equality is true.

 </details>

Now, we shall construct some properties of the probability function from the axiom of probability. First we construct properties with a single set.

**Theorem 1.6** If $P$ is a probability function and $A$ is any set in $\mathcal{B}$, then

1. $P(\emptyset)=0$ ;
2. $P(A)\le1$ ;
3. $P(A^c)=1-P(A)$.
