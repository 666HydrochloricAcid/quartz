Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900) stands at a turning point in this series. Most of what came before him, from [[Plato]] to [[Arthur Schopenhauer|Schopenhauer]], appears in his work as something to be diagnosed. Much of what came after him begins by deciding what to make of him: [[Martin Heidegger|Heidegger]]'s history of being, [[Karl Jaspers|Jaspers]] and [[Albert Camus|Camus]] on existence, [[Michel Foucault|Foucault]]'s genealogies, [[Gilles Deleuze|Deleuze]]'s philosophy of difference.

He is also unusually hard to summarize. He wrote in aphorisms, in polemics, and, in *Zarathustra*, in something close to scripture. He revised his views from book to book, and he left behind thousands of pages of notes whose authority is still disputed. Rather than survey everything, this post follows a single thread. Nietzsche's problem is *nihilism*, the devaluation of the highest values that follows the "death of God." His critical work (the history of the "true world," the genealogy of morals, perspectivism) diagnoses where those values came from and why they are failing. His positive doctrines (will to power, eternal recurrence, the Übermensch) are attempts to answer the question that remains once the diagnosis is accepted: how can a life be affirmed without appeal to anything beyond it?

## Life

Nietzsche was born on 15 October 1844 in Röcken, in Prussian Saxony. His father, a Lutheran pastor, died when he was four. After the boarding school of Schulpforta he studied classical philology at Bonn and Leipzig, and in 1869, at twenty-four and before he had completed his doctorate, he was appointed professor of classical philology at Basel. What followed is customarily divided into three periods.

- **Early (1869–1876).** Under the spell of Schopenhauer and of Richard Wagner, whom he befriended: *The Birth of Tragedy* and the *Untimely Meditations*.
- **Middle (1876–1882).** The break with Wagner and a turn toward science and psychological analysis, sometimes called his positivist period: *Human, All Too Human*, *Daybreak*, and the first four books of *The Gay Science*. Ill health forced him to resign from Basel in 1879, and he spent the next decade moving between rented rooms in Sils-Maria, Genoa, Nice, and Turin.
- **Late (1883–1888).** *Zarathustra*, *Beyond Good and Evil*, the *Genealogy*, and six further works written in 1888 alone.

In January 1889 he collapsed in Turin and never recovered. He spent his last eleven years incapacitated, cared for first by his mother and then by his sister Elisabeth, and died in Weimar on 25 August 1900.

Elisabeth matters for how Nietzsche is read. She founded the Nietzsche-Archiv, took control of his notes, and assembled from them a book titled *Der Wille zur Macht* (1901; expanded 1906), which she presented as his unfinished magnum opus. She also steered his reputation toward German nationalism and, later, National Socialism, although Nietzsche had despised antisemitism and had quarreled bitterly with her over her antisemitic husband, Bernhard Förster. The critical edition of Giorgio Colli and Mazzino Montinari, begun in 1967, restored the notes to their chronological order, and scholars now cite individual fragments rather than Elisabeth's compilation.

## Starting point: Schopenhauer and the Greeks

Nietzsche's first book argues that Attic tragedy was born from the union of two artistic drives, which he names after two gods. The *Apollonian* is the drive toward form, measure, and the clear outline of the individual: the world as dream-image, governed by what Schopenhauer had called the *principium individuationis*. The *Dionysian* is the drive toward intoxication, in which the boundaries of the individual dissolve and one feels absorbed into a primordial unity. The Greeks, on this account, were not serene. They knew the wisdom of Silenus, that the best thing for a human being is never to have been born and the second best is to die soon (GT §3), and tragedy was the form in which they could look at this truth without being destroyed by it.

> nur als ästhetisches Phänomen ist das Dasein und die Welt ewig gerechtfertigt
>
> only as an aesthetic phenomenon are existence and the world eternally justified
>
> (GT §5)

The sentence already marks a departure from Schopenhauer. For Schopenhauer, insight into the will behind appearances leads to resignation: the wise turn away from life and deny the will to live. For Nietzsche, the Greeks show that the same insight can coexist with affirmation, and can even intensify it. In the 1886 preface "Attempt at a Self-Criticism," Nietzsche regretted having expressed this in Kantian and Schopenhauerian formulas, but the question itself, how to affirm existence without metaphysical consolation, runs through his whole work.

The book also introduces his first great antagonist. Tragedy, he claims, died at the hands of Euripides, behind whom spoke a newborn daemon called Socrates (GT §12). [[Socrates]] stands for theoretical optimism, the faith that thought can penetrate being and even correct it, and he remains a target to the end (see "The Problem of Socrates" in GD). Nietzsche's admiration for the philosophers before Socrates never faded. The unfinished *Philosophy in the Tragic Age of the Greeks* (1873) has chapters on [[Milesian School#Thales|Thales]], [[Milesian School#Anaximander|Anaximander]], [[Heraclitus]], [[Eleatic School#Parmenides|Parmenides]], and [[Pluralist School#Anaxagoras|Anaxagoras]], and Heraclitus, with his world of becoming and strife, is the predecessor Nietzsche acknowledged most readily.

## The death of God

The most famous passage in Nietzsche is a parable. In FW §125 a madman lights a lantern in the bright morning, runs into the marketplace, and cries that he is looking for God. The bystanders, who, Nietzsche stresses, do not believe in God, laugh at him. He turns on them:

> Gott ist tot! Gott bleibt tot! Und wir haben ihn getötet!
>
> God is dead! God remains dead! And we have killed him!
>
> (FW §125)

Three features of the parable are easy to miss.

First, it is addressed to unbelievers. Nietzsche is not offering an argument that God does not exist; the people in the marketplace have already concluded that. He is describing an event in a culture. As he puts it at the opening of Book V, the belief in the Christian God has become unbelievable (FW §343).

Second, "we have killed him." The murderer is not an external enemy of faith but something Christianity itself cultivated: the will to truth, the demand for intellectual honesty, which eventually turned on the faith that had trained it. Nietzsche calls this the self-overcoming of Christian morality (GM III §27).

Third, the madman concludes that he has come too early. The deed has been done, but its consequences have not yet reached those who did it. The bystanders think they can drop God and keep everything else. Nietzsche mocks the same attitude in George Eliot and the English moralists, who believed they could keep Christian morality without the Christian God (GD "Skirmishes of an Untimely Man" §5). If the highest values were grounded in God, his death leaves them groundless, whether or not anyone has noticed yet.

The name for what follows is nihilism.

> Was bedeutet Nihilismus? — Dass die obersten Werte sich entwerten.
>
> What does nihilism mean? That the highest values devalue themselves.
>
> (KSA 12, 9[35])

In the same note Nietzsche distinguishes two forms. *Passive* nihilism is exhaustion: values collapse and nothing replaces them. *Active* nihilism is a sign of increased strength: it destroys what has become untenable in order to make room. The rest of his work can be read as an effort to pass from the first to the second, and then beyond nihilism altogether.

## How the "true world" became a fable

*Twilight of the Idols* contains a one-page history of Western metaphysics, subtitled "History of an Error." It has six stages, each followed by a gloss in parentheses. Condensed:

1. The true world, attainable for the wise, the pious, the virtuous; he lives in it, *he is it*. (The oldest form of the idea, simple and convincing: a paraphrase of "I, Plato, am the truth.")
2. The true world, unattainable for now, but promised to the wise, the pious, the virtuous ("to the sinner who repents"). (Progress of the idea: it becomes subtler, more insidious, harder to grasp; it becomes Christian.)
3. The true world, unattainable, unprovable, unpromisable, but even as a mere thought a consolation, an obligation, an imperative. (The old sun, but seen through fog and skepticism; the idea grown sublime, pale, Nordic, Königsbergian.)
4. The true world: unattainable? In any case unattained, and as unattained also *unknown*. Hence no longer consoling, redeeming, or obligating: how could something unknown obligate us? (Grey morning. First yawn of reason. Cockcrow of positivism.)
5. The "true world": an idea no longer good for anything, not even for obligating; a superfluous idea, *therefore* a refuted one. Let us abolish it! (Broad daylight; breakfast; return of good sense and cheerfulness; Plato blushes with shame; uproar of all free spirits.)
6. The last stage, in full:

> Die wahre Welt haben wir abgeschafft: welche Welt blieb übrig? die scheinbare vielleicht? … Aber nein! mit der wahren Welt haben wir auch die scheinbare abgeschafft!
>
> The true world we have abolished: what world remained? The apparent one, perhaps? … But no! With the true world we have also abolished the apparent one!
>
> (GD "How the 'True World' Finally Became a Fable")

Its gloss: noon, the moment of the shortest shadow, the end of the longest error, the high point of humanity; *incipit Zarathustra*.

The stages can be matched with figures in this series: [[Plato]]; Christianity, from [[Paul the Apostle|Paul]] to [[Augustine of Hippo|Augustine]]; [[Immanuel Kant|Kant]], for whom the thing in itself is unknowable but the moral law still commands; positivism; Nietzsche's own middle period and its "free spirits"; and finally Zarathustra.

The decisive step is the last one. Stage 5 is the empiricist's victory: the supersensible world is dropped and only the world of the senses remains. Stage 6 says that this victory is illusory. "Apparent" meant "less real than the true world." Once the true world goes, the word "apparent" loses the term it was contrasted with, and the whole two-tier picture goes with it. Nietzsche is not saying that only appearances exist. He is saying that the opposition between being and appearance, which has organized metaphysics since Plato, can no longer be drawn.

This is also why the death of God and the end of metaphysics are, for Nietzsche, one event seen from two sides. In the preface to *Beyond Good and Evil* he calls Christianity "Platonismus fürs Volk," Platonism for the people: the two-world picture in popular form. When belief in God becomes unbelievable, the metaphysical true world loses its last support, and conversely.

[[Martin Heidegger|Heidegger]] read this passage closely in his lectures on Nietzsche and drew a sharper conclusion: in overturning Platonism, Nietzsche remained bound to it, and so completed metaphysics rather than escaping it. Whether that verdict is fair is one of the central questions of twentieth-century Nietzsche interpretation.

## Genealogy: the value of values

If the highest values are losing their force, one can ask a question that would earlier have seemed impious.

> der Wert dieser Werte ist selbst erst einmal in Frage zu stellen
>
> the value of these values must itself, for once, be called into question
>
> (GM Preface §6)

Genealogy asks not whether a moral claim is true but where a value came from, what need it answered, and what kind of life it promotes. This invites the objection that it commits the genetic fallacy, since the origin of a belief does not settle its validity. But Nietzsche's targets present themselves as timeless and unconditioned. Showing that they have a history, and an unflattering one, is already a way of showing that they could have been otherwise. The *Genealogy* consists of three essays.

**First essay: "Good and Evil," "Good and Bad."** There are two ways of producing a pair of opposed values. In *noble* valuation, "good" comes first: the strong call themselves good as an expression of their own flourishing, and "bad" is an afterthought for whatever is unlike them. In *slave* valuation the order is reversed. The first concept is "evil," aimed at the powerful who cause suffering, and "good" is derived as its negation: the good man is the one who does no harm, who is meek, patient, and humble.

> Der Sklavenaufstand in der Moral beginnt damit, dass das Ressentiment selbst schöpferisch wird und Werte gebiert
>
> The slave revolt in morality begins when ressentiment itself becomes creative and gives birth to values
>
> (GM I §10)

The point is structural. Noble morality begins with a Yes to itself, slave morality with a No to something outside it. *Ressentiment*, the resentment of those who cannot strike back, takes an imaginary revenge instead: it reinterprets weakness as virtue and strength as guilt. Nietzsche locates the beginning of this revolt in the priestly religion of ancient Judaism and its triumph in Christianity.

**Second essay: guilt and bad conscience.** The German *Schuld* means both guilt and debt, and Nietzsche derives the moral concept from the economic one: the relation between creditor and debtor, in which suffering can be exacted as repayment (GM II §4–8). Bad conscience proper arises when human beings are enclosed in society and their aggressive instincts can no longer discharge outward, so they turn inward. Nietzsche calls this the internalization (*Verinnerlichung*) of man (GM II §16), the origin of the "soul" as an inner space.

**Third essay: the ascetic ideal.** Why have priests, philosophers, and saints so consistently valued poverty, chastity, and self-denial? Because the ascetic ideal gives suffering a meaning. Human beings can bear almost any suffering, Nietzsche says, but not suffering that is meaningless. The ascetic ideal interpreted suffering as guilt and so made it intelligible, at the cost of turning the will against life itself. The book ends:

> lieber will noch der Mensch das Nichts wollen, als nicht wollen
>
> man would rather will nothingness than not will
>
> (GM III §28)

This sentence is the hinge between the critique and what follows. Even nihilism is still willing. The will does not stop; it can only change direction. Whatever answer Nietzsche gives to nihilism must therefore come from the will itself. [[Michel Foucault|Foucault]]'s histories of madness, punishment, and sexuality take their method from this book.

## Will to power

In *Zarathustra* the doctrine is announced as a direct correction of Schopenhauer:

> Nur, wo Leben ist, da ist auch Wille: aber nicht Wille zum Leben, sondern — so lehre ich's dich — Wille zur Macht!
>
> Only where there is life is there also will: not will to life, but (so I teach you) will to power!
>
> (Za II "On Self-Overcoming")

Schopenhauer's will is a will to *live*: blind, insatiable striving for existence, whose satisfaction is only the brief absence of pain. Nietzsche's will to power is not a desire for political domination. It is a claim about what living things do. They do not primarily seek to preserve themselves; they seek to expand, to discharge their strength, to overcome resistance. In JGB §13 he turns this against the physiologists, and explicitly against [[Baruch Spinoza|Spinoza]], who make self-preservation the cardinal drive of organisms. "Leben selbst ist Wille zur Macht," life itself is will to power, and self-preservation is only one of its indirect and most frequent consequences.

In JGB §36 Nietzsche goes further, and in the conditional mood. *Suppose* that nothing is given as real except our world of desires and passions, and suppose we tried to explain the mechanical world, too, by this one kind of causality. Then the world seen from within

> sie wäre eben „Wille zur Macht" und nichts außerdem
>
> would be precisely "will to power" and nothing besides
>
> (JGB §36)

Readers have divided over how seriously to take the conditional. [[Martin Heidegger|Heidegger]] treated the will to power as Nietzsche's answer to the oldest metaphysical question, what beings as a whole are, and therefore as the last metaphysics of the West. Readers in the naturalist and psychological tradition, from Walter Kaufmann onward, take it primarily as a hypothesis about human motivation, extended to the world only experimentally, and note that in the published works the cosmological version appears only as a hypothesis.

## Perspectivism

> Es gibt nur ein perspektivisches Sehen, nur ein perspektivisches „Erkennen"
>
> There is only a perspectival seeing, only a perspectival "knowing"
>
> (GM III §12)

Nietzsche continues: the more affects we allow to speak about a thing, and the more eyes, different eyes, we can bring to bear on it, the more complete our "concept" of it, our "objectivity," will be. His target is the idea of a knower without a perspective, a pure, will-less, painless, timeless subject of knowledge. (The phrase echoes Schopenhauer's account of aesthetic contemplation.) Nietzsche compares such a subject to an eye that looks in no direction at all, which is an absurdity. Objectivity, on this view, is not the absence of perspective but the ability to command many perspectives.

In a note aimed at positivism, which stops at phenomena and says that there are only facts, he puts it more bluntly:

> nein, gerade Tatsachen gibt es nicht, nur Interpretationen
>
> no, facts are precisely what there are not, only interpretations
>
> (KSA 12, 7[60])

The obvious objection is self-referential: if there are only interpretations, then this claim too is only an interpretation. Nietzsche anticipates the objection and welcomes it.

> Gesetzt, dass auch dies nur Interpretation ist — und ihr werdet eifrig genug sein, dies einzuwenden? — nun, um so besser.
>
> Supposing that this too is only interpretation (and you will be eager enough to raise that objection?), well, so much the better.
>
> (JGB §22)

The reply is less evasive than it looks. Nietzsche does not claim to occupy a standpoint outside all perspectives; that is exactly what he denies anyone can do. His wager is that interpretations can still be ranked, by how much they can see, how many perspectives they can incorporate, and what kind of life they make possible, without any of them being a view from nowhere. [[Immanuel Kant|Kant]]'s thing in itself, which Nietzsche lists alongside "immediate certainty" and "absolute knowledge" as a contradiction in terms (JGB §16), is the clearest example of what he gives up.

## Eternal recurrence

In its first edition, *The Gay Science* ended with two sections. The second, §342, is the opening of *Zarathustra*. The first, §341, introduces the thought that Zarathustra will come to teach. It is framed as a question: what if, one day or night, a demon crept after you into your loneliest loneliness and said

> Dieses Leben, wie du es jetzt lebst und gelebt hast, wirst du noch einmal und noch unzählige Male leben müssen
>
> This life, as you now live it and have lived it, you will have to live once more and innumerable times more
>
> (FW §341)

and added that every pain and every joy, every thought and sigh, everything unspeakably small or great in your life would return, all in the same order and sequence? Would you throw yourself down, gnash your teeth, and curse the demon? Or have you once experienced a moment so tremendous that you would answer that he is a god and you have never heard anything more divine? The question "do you want this once more and innumerable times more?" would then lie on every action as the heaviest weight.

The passage is a thought experiment, not an argument, and on the most common reading the doctrine is primarily practical: a test of one's relation to one's own life. The test is unforgiving. It does not ask whether one's life was good on balance, or whether its sufferings were redeemed by some later reward. It asks whether one can will the whole of it, pains included, exactly as it was. This is Nietzsche's answer to the nihilism of the ascetic ideal. The ascetic ideal justified suffering by pointing beyond life; recurrence removes the beyond and asks whether life can be affirmed anyway.

The attitude that passes the test, Nietzsche calls by a Latin name:

> Meine Formel für die Größe am Menschen ist amor fati: dass man nichts anders haben will, vorwärts nicht, rückwärts nicht, in alle Ewigkeit nicht.
>
> My formula for greatness in a human being is amor fati: that one wants nothing to be different, not forward, not backward, not in all eternity.
>
> (EH "Why I Am So Clever" §10)

In *Zarathustra* the thought is treated as almost unbearable. In "On the Vision and the Riddle" (Za III), Zarathustra stands at a gateway named *Moment*, where two paths, one running back into eternity and one forward, meet. The vision ends with a shepherd choking on a heavy black snake; he bites off its head, spits it out, and rises laughing, transformed.

### Remark: the cosmological version

In his notes, though not in the published works, Nietzsche also tried to prove recurrence as a fact about the world (KSA 13, 14[188]). The argument runs roughly as follows. The world is a determinate quantity of force with a determinate number of centers of force. It therefore passes through a calculable number of combinations. Time is infinite. Hence every possible combination must already have been realized, and realized infinitely often: the world is a circular course that has already repeated itself infinitely many times.

Under its assumptions the argument is valid, and it is worth seeing exactly why.

**Proposition.** Let $S$ be a finite set and $f : S \to S$ a map. Suppose the present state $x_0 \in S$ has an infinite past, that is, there are $x_{-1}, x_{-2}, \dots \in S$ with $f(x_{-(n+1)}) = x_{-n}$ for all $n \ge 0$. Then $x_0$ is periodic: $f^p(x_0) = x_0$ for some $p \ge 1$.

**Proof.** Since $S$ is finite, the terms of $(x_{-n})_{n \ge 0}$ cannot all be distinct, so $x_{-i} = x_{-j}$ for some $0 \le i < j$. Put $p = j - i$. Then $f^p(x_{-j}) = x_{-i} = x_{-j}$, and since $x_0 = f^j(x_{-j})$,
$$
f^p(x_0) = f^j\big(f^p(x_{-j})\big) = f^j(x_{-j}) = x_0. \qquad \blacksquare
$$

Both hypotheses are needed. Without an infinite past, the present can be a transient state that never returns: on $S = \{a, b\}$ with $f(a) = f(b) = b$, the state $a$ occurs only once. This is why Nietzsche insists that time is infinite in the past, and why he argues in the same note that if the world could reach a final state, it would already have reached it.

The weak point is finiteness. Georg Simmel (*Schopenhauer und Nietzsche*, 1907) gave a counterexample with only three moving parts. Take three wheels of equal size on a common axis, each with a marked point, the three points initially in a line, rotating with angular velocities $\omega$, $2\omega$, and $\omega/\pi$. The initial alignment recurs at a time $t > 0$ only if $\omega t \in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}$ and $\omega t/\pi \in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}$, that is, $\omega t = 2\pi m = 2\pi^2 n$ for positive integers $m, n$. Then $\pi = m/n$, contradicting the irrationality of $\pi$. (The second wheel plays no role.)

What does hold is approximate recurrence. The pair of angles $(\omega t, \omega t/\pi) \bmod 2\pi$ traces a line of irrational slope on the torus $\mathbb{T}^2$, which is dense, so the system comes back arbitrarily close to its initial state infinitely often. This is an instance of the phenomenon described in general by Poincaré's recurrence theorem (1890): if $T$ is a measure-preserving transformation of a probability space $(X, \mathcal{F}, \mu)$ and $A \in \mathcal{F}$ with $\mu(A) > 0$, then $\mu$-almost every point of $A$ returns to $A$ infinitely often. But approximate recurrence is not what the demon promises. "All in the same order and sequence" demands exact identity, and exact identity is no longer guaranteed once the state space is a continuum. %% link: Poincaré recurrence post in Measure Theory %%

## The Übermensch

Zarathustra comes down from the mountains after ten years of solitude and, in the marketplace of the first town he reaches, delivers his first speech:

> Ich lehre euch den Übermenschen. Der Mensch ist etwas, das überwunden werden soll.
>
> I teach you the Übermensch. Man is something that shall be overcome.
>
> (Za Prologue §3)

And a little later:

> Der Mensch ist ein Seil, geknüpft zwischen Tier und Übermensch — ein Seil über einem Abgrunde.
>
> Man is a rope, tied between beast and Übermensch: a rope over an abyss.
>
> (Za Prologue §4)

The image of the rope matters more than the name. What is great in man, Zarathustra says in the same section, is that he is a bridge and not an end. Nietzsche's language is that of overcoming (*Überwindung*), and the Übermensch is defined less by any positive content than as the one who no longer needs the old values in order to affirm life. It is not a biological species and not a master race, and the word almost disappears from Nietzsche's books after *Zarathustra*.

The crowd laughs, so Zarathustra describes the most contemptible thing instead: the *last man*, who has made everything small and comfortable, has no longing, wants neither to rule nor to obey, and holds that everyone is the same.

> „Wir haben das Glück erfunden" — sagen die letzten Menschen und blinzeln.
>
> "We have invented happiness," say the last men, and they blink.
>
> (Za Prologue §5)

The last man is passive nihilism made comfortable. The crowd's response is telling: they shout that Zarathustra should keep his Übermensch and give them the last man instead.

### The three metamorphoses

Zarathustra's first speech proper, "On the Three Metamorphoses" (Za I), compresses the whole movement into three images.

- **The camel.** The spirit first becomes a beast of burden. It kneels to be loaded with the heaviest things, reverence and duty, and hurries with them into the desert.
- **The lion.** In the loneliest desert the camel becomes a lion, which wants to be master in its own desert. It confronts a great dragon whose name is "Thou shalt"; on every one of its golden scales glitters a "Thou shalt," the values of millennia. Against it the lion says "I will." The lion cannot yet create new values, but it can create freedom for new creation, including a sacred No even to duty.
- **The child.** Finally the lion becomes a child.

> Unschuld ist das Kind und Vergessen, ein Neubeginnen, ein Spiel, ein aus sich rollendes Rad, eine erste Bewegung, ein heiliges Ja-sagen.
>
> The child is innocence and forgetting, a new beginning, a game, a self-propelled wheel, a first movement, a sacred Yes-saying.
>
> (Za I "On the Three Metamorphoses")

Read against the rest of this post, the camel is morality as received; the lion is the critical work, the death of God, the destruction of the true world, and genealogy; and the child is what Nietzsche hoped would come after, the creation of values and the affirmation tested by recurrence. The lion's limitation is the important point. Critique can free us from the old values, but it cannot by itself produce new ones. A purely critical Nietzsche would be only an active nihilist.

## After Nietzsche

- Elisabeth's archive and the Nazi appropriation shaped his reputation for half a century. In English, Walter Kaufmann's *Nietzsche: Philosopher, Psychologist, Antichrist* (1950) did most to undo it.
- [[Karl Jaspers|Jaspers]] (*Nietzsche*, 1936) read him, together with [[Søren Kierkegaard|Kierkegaard]], as one of the two great exceptions whose existence marks the crisis of reason.
- [[Martin Heidegger|Heidegger]]'s lectures of 1936–1940 (published 1961) made him the last metaphysician, in whom the history of being from Plato onward reaches its end.
- [[Albert Camus|Camus]]'s *The Myth of Sisyphus* takes up the question of how to live without appeal to anything beyond life. Its closing image of a happy Sisyphus stands close to amor fati, though Camus grounds it in lucid revolt rather than in love of fate.
- [[Gilles Deleuze|Deleuze]] (*Nietzsche et la philosophie*, 1962) read eternal recurrence as the return not of the same but of difference, and turned Nietzsche against Hegel.
- [[Michel Foucault|Foucault]] ("Nietzsche, la généalogie, l'histoire," 1971) made genealogy a method for history.
- [[Sigmund Freud|Freud]] said he had long avoided reading Nietzsche in order to keep his own work independent. The overlap (the drives, repression, the internalization of aggression) has been noted ever since.

## Reading order

For the primary texts, a reasonable order is GM (the most sustained argument), then JGB, GD, and FW (especially Book V and §§125 and 341), and only then *Zarathustra*, which presupposes nearly everything else.
