Arthur Schopenhauer (1788–1860) is the one philosopher in this series' line of German idealism who claimed both to keep [[Immanuel Kant|Kant]]'s boundary and to know what lies behind it. [[Fichte and Schelling]] and [[Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel|Hegel]], whom he despised, dissolved the thing in itself into the activity of reason. Schopenhauer kept it and gave it a name: will. This will is not reason and has no purpose. It is a blind, endless striving that appears in the fall of a stone, the growth of a plant, the instincts of an animal, and the desires of a human being, and since it can never be satisfied, life is essentially suffering. Schopenhauer was the first major Western philosopher to place Indian thought at the center of his work and the first to make pessimism a philosophical position, and no philosopher has ranked music higher. In this series he leads directly to [[Friedrich Nietzsche|Nietzsche]], who began as his disciple and became his critic, to [[Sigmund Freud|Freud]], who acknowledged that Schopenhauer had anticipated the theory of repression, and to the young [[Ludwig Wittgenstein|Wittgenstein]].

This post follows what Schopenhauer called the single thought of his main work, *The World as Will and Representation*: the world that appears to us as representation is in itself will, and this will, once seen through, can turn against itself. The four books of the first volume unfold the thought from four sides. Book I treats the world as representation, governed by the principle of sufficient reason. Book II treats the world as will, known from within through one's own body. Book III treats the Platonic Ideas and the art that presents them, in which the will falls silent for a moment. Book IV treats ethics: the suffering inseparable from willing, the compassion that sees through individuality, and the denial of the will to life. The Preface says how the book is meant to be read.

> Was durch dasselbe mitgeteilt werden soll, ist ein einziger Gedanke. Dennoch konnte ich, aller Bemühungen ungeachtet, keinen kürzeren Weg ihn mitzuteilen finden als dieses ganze Buch.
>
> What is to be communicated by it is a single thought. Yet despite all my efforts I could find no shorter way of communicating it than this whole book.
>
> (WWV I, Preface to the first edition)

The book, he added, should therefore be read twice, since its beginning presupposes its end almost as much as its end presupposes its beginning.

## Life

Schopenhauer was born on 22 February 1788 in Danzig, the son of Heinrich Floris Schopenhauer, a wealthy merchant, and Johanna Trosiener, who later became one of the most widely read novelists in Germany. His father chose the name Arthur because it was spelled the same in the major European languages. When Danzig was annexed by Prussia in 1793, the family moved to the free city of Hamburg. Arthur spent two years with a business associate's family in Le Havre, learning French, and at fifteen he was offered a choice: he could enter a Gymnasium and prepare for a scholarly career, or accompany his parents on a long tour of Europe and then become a merchant. He chose the tour (1803–1804), which took him through England, France, Switzerland, and Austria, and then began a commercial apprenticeship in Hamburg. His father died in 1805. His mother moved to Weimar, where she opened a salon that Goethe frequented, and in 1807 she let Arthur leave commerce for school.

He studied at Göttingen from 1809, first medicine and then philosophy, under Gottlob Ernst Schulze, the author of the skeptical *Aenesidemus*, who advised him to study Plato and Kant before anyone else. In 1811 Christoph Martin Wieland, an old friend of his mother's, tried to talk him out of so unpractical a subject. Schopenhauer later recorded his answer:

> Das Leben ist eine missliche Sache, ich habe mir vorgesetzt, es damit hinzubringen, über dasselbe nachzudenken.
>
> Life is a miserable business; I have resolved to spend it in thinking about it.
>
> (Conversation with Wieland, 1811)

He heard Fichte and Schleiermacher in Berlin (1811–1813), wrote his dissertation on the principle of sufficient reason in Rudolstadt while the war of liberation against Napoleon passed by, and received his doctorate from Jena in 1813. Back in Weimar he worked with Goethe on the theory of colors, a collaboration that produced *On Vision and Colors* (1816) and some coolness when the younger man claimed to have improved on the older man's theory. In the same winter the orientalist Friedrich Majer introduced him to Indian thought. After a final quarrel with his mother in 1814 he never saw her again, and he moved to Dresden, where in four years he wrote *The World as Will and Representation*. It appeared at the end of 1818, dated 1819, and found almost no readers.

In 1820 he habilitated at Berlin and announced his lectures for the same hour as Hegel's principal course. Few students came, and his later announcements were rarely followed by lectures. The cholera epidemic of 1831, which killed Hegel in November, drove Schopenhauer from Berlin in August, and from 1833 he lived in Frankfurt am Main on his inheritance, with a fixed routine, a flute, and a succession of poodles. He published *On the Will in Nature* (1836) and won a prize of the Royal Norwegian Society of Sciences for his essay on the freedom of the will (1839). The Royal Danish Society refused to crown his essay on the basis of morality (1840), although it was the only entry, objecting among other things to his treatment of the most eminent philosophers of recent times. A second edition of the main work, with a second volume of supplements, followed in 1844. Recognition came only with *Parerga and Paralipomena* (1851), a collection of essays for which the publisher paid him nothing, and with an anonymous article by John Oxenford in the *Westminster Review* (1853), "Iconoclasm in German Philosophy." Schopenhauer watched what he called the comedy of his fame for seven more years and died in Frankfurt on 21 September 1860. He left his estate to a fund for Prussian soldiers disabled in suppressing the revolutions of 1848–1849, a last expression of the political conservatism that had made him lend his opera glass to an officer who wanted to observe the insurgents on the Frankfurt barricades from his window. His work falls into three periods, although he himself held that everything essential was in place by 1818.

- **Formation (1809–1814).** Göttingen, Berlin, and Weimar: the dissertation, the work with Goethe on colors, and the first encounter with Indian thought.
- **The main work (1814–1818).** Dresden: *The World as Will and Representation*.
- **Supplements (1819–1860).** Berlin and Frankfurt: the essays on the will in nature, on freedom, and on morality, the second volume, and *Parerga and Paralipomena*.

Unlike Hegel and Nietzsche, Schopenhauer left his texts in order. He revised the main work twice himself and left notes for a further edition; his literary executor Julius Frauenstädt published the collected works in 1873–1874, and the edition of Arthur Hübscher is now standard.

## Starting point: Kant, Plato, and the Upanishads

Schopenhauer named his sources in the Preface to the first edition. The reader of his book must know Kant's principal writings. If he has also lingered in the school of the divine Plato, he will be better prepared, and if he has shared in the benefit of the Vedas, opened to Europe through the Upanishads, he will be best prepared of all (WWV I, Preface to the first edition). Each source supplied one element of the system.

From [[Immanuel Kant|Kant]] came the frame. The appendix to the first volume, a long "Criticism of the Kantian Philosophy," opens with a verdict:

> Kants größtes Verdienst ist die Unterscheidung der Erscheinung vom Dinge an sich
>
> Kant's greatest merit is the distinction between the appearance and the thing in itself
>
> (WWV I, Appendix)

The rest of Kant fared worse. Schopenhauer kept the Transcendental Aesthetic, reduced the twelve categories to the one he thought indispensable, causality, and rejected Kant's moral philosophy almost entirely. He also accepted the objection, discussed in the Kant post in connection with Jacobi, that his teacher Schulze had pressed: Kant made the thing in itself the cause of our sensations, although causality is valid only among appearances. Schopenhauer drew the consequence that the thing in itself cannot be reached by any inference from appearances. He also blamed the second edition of the *Critique* for retreating from the idealism of the first, and in 1837 he persuaded the editors of a new collected edition of Kant to print the first-edition text.

From [[Plato]] came the Ideas, which Schopenhauer reinterpreted as the eternal grades at which the one will becomes object: the forms of natural forces and of species, which are known not by concepts but in contemplation. From the Upanishads, which he read in Anquetil-Duperron's Latin version of a Persian translation, the *Oupnek'hat* (1801–1802), came the thought that the multiplicity of individuals is an illusion. Late in life he called the book the most rewarding and elevating reading possible in the world, apart from the original:

> sie ist der Trost meines Lebens gewesen und wird der meines Sterbens sein.
>
> it has been the consolation of my life and will be the consolation of my death.
>
> (P II §185)

The idealists, finally, supplied him with enemies. Fichte, whose lectures he heard in Berlin, Schelling, and above all Hegel he dismissed for the rest of his life as sophists who had betrayed Kant.

## The world as representation

The first book begins:

> „Die Welt ist meine Vorstellung": – dies ist eine Wahrheit, welche in Beziehung auf jedes lebende und erkennende Wesen gilt, wiewohl der Mensch allein sie in das reflektierte abstrakte Bewusstsein bringen kann.
>
> "The world is my representation": this is a truth that holds for every living and knowing being, although the human being alone can bring it into reflective, abstract consciousness.
>
> (WWV I §1)

Whatever exists for knowledge is object only in relation to a subject. The subject is "that which knows everything and is known by none" (§2); it is not in space and time, which are its forms. There is no object without a subject and no subject without an object. Objects in turn are governed throughout by a single principle, the principle of sufficient reason, which Schopenhauer's dissertation had traced to four roots, one for each class of objects.

| Root | Class of objects | Form of the principle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Becoming (*fiendi*) | Intuitive, empirical representations | Every change has a cause |
| Knowing (*cognoscendi*) | Concepts, abstract representations | Every true judgment has a ground |
| Being (*essendi*) | The pure intuitions of space and time | Every position and every moment is determined by others |
| Acting (*agendi*) | The subject of willing | Every action has a motive |

The root of becoming is causality, and the understanding, for Schopenhauer, is nothing but the capacity to apply it. Perception is already intellectual: the understanding refers sensations to their causes and so produces the intuited world (§4). Animals therefore have understanding too. Only human beings have reason, the faculty of abstract concepts, which gives them language, science, and deliberation (§8). Book I ends with a portrait of reason's most complete practical use, [[Stoicism|Stoic]] ethics, whose sage Schopenhauer judged a contradiction, a stiff wooden mannequin with whom nothing can be done (§16).

The root of being grounds mathematics, and it led Schopenhauer to an unusual view of proof. A geometrical theorem holds because positions in space determine one another, and this ground of being can be seen in intuition; a logical proof from axioms shows only that the theorem holds, not why. He accordingly called Euclid's proof of the Pythagorean theorem "stilted, indeed underhanded" and offered instead a figure for the isosceles case: the square on the hypotenuse, divided by its diagonals into four triangles congruent to the given one, while each square on a leg is divided by one diagonal into two (§15). It is essentially the figure Socrates draws for the slave boy in Plato's *Meno*.

The world so described is real for experience and nothing more, and Schopenhauer gave this thesis an Indian name.

> Es ist die Maja, der Schleier des Truges, welcher die Augen der Sterblichen umhüllt und sie eine Welt sehen lässt, von der man weder sagen kann, dass sie sei, noch auch, dass sie nicht sei
>
> It is Maya, the veil of deception, which covers the eyes of mortals and lets them see a world of which one can say neither that it is nor that it is not
>
> (WWV I §3)

Life and dreams, he adds, are leaves of one and the same book (§5). Against materialism, which explains the subject as a product of matter, he objected that matter is itself only a representation of the subject, so that the materialist resembles Baron Münchhausen, who pulls himself and his horse out of the water by his own pigtail (§7).

## The world as will

Natural science, however far it is carried, explains only how appearances are connected, never what they are. Its explanations end in forces of nature, such as gravity, electricity, and chemical affinity, which it can name and measure but not understand.

> … dass man von außen dem Wesen der Dinge nimmermehr beikommen kann: wie man auch forschen mag, so gewinnt man nichts als Bilder und Namen.
>
> … that from outside one can never get at the inner nature of things: however one may investigate, one gains nothing but images and names.
>
> (WWV I §17)

Inquiry of this kind, Schopenhauer says, is like someone who walks round a castle looking in vain for an entrance and sketching the façades in the meantime (§17).

There is one object, however, that each of us knows in a second way. The knowing subject is also an individual, rooted in the world through a body, and the body is given to it twice: as a representation among representations, an object in space subject to the laws of all objects, and at the same time as something known immediately from within, which each of us calls will.

> Der Willensakt und die Aktion des Leibes sind nicht zwei objektiv erkannte verschiedene Zustände, die das Band der Kausalität verknüpft, stehen nicht im Verhältnis der Ursache und Wirkung; sondern sie sind eines und dasselbe, nur auf zwei gänzlich verschiedene Weisen gegeben: einmal ganz unmittelbar und einmal in der Anschauung für den Verstand.
>
> The act of will and the action of the body are not two different states known objectively and connected by the bond of causality; they do not stand in the relation of cause and effect. They are one and the same, only given in two entirely different ways: once quite immediately, and once in intuition for the understanding.
>
> (WWV I §18)

The body is the will made visible. Schopenhauer then extends this key to every other appearance. To deny that other things have an inner nature analogous to ours would be theoretical egoism, a position that, like a small border fortress, cannot be taken, but whose garrison can never sally forth from it either (§19). If everything has an inner side, that side must be what we know in ourselves as will, and Schopenhauer names the whole after its clearest appearance. The force that makes a crystal form, a magnet turn to the north, and a stone fall is the same will that moves us, at a lower grade of visibility (§§21–23).

> Spinoza (epist. 62) sagt, dass der durch einen Stoß in die Luft fliegende Stein, wenn er Bewusstsein hätte, meinen würde, aus seinem eigenen Willen zu fliegen. Ich setze nur noch hinzu, dass der Stein recht hätte.
>
> Spinoza (epist. 62) says that a stone flying through the air because of a push would, if it had consciousness, think that it was flying of its own will. I add only that the stone would be right.
>
> (WWV I §24)

[[Baruch Spinoza|Spinoza]] had meant the example as an illustration of the illusion of free will. Schopenhauer turns it into a metaphysics. The will so understood is not a faculty of a mind and has nothing to do with intellect. It is groundless, since the principle of sufficient reason governs only its appearances. It is one, since plurality exists only in space and time, which together form the *principium individuationis*. And it is without goal: "absence of all aim, of all limits, belongs to the essence of the will in itself, which is an endless striving" (§29). Its objectification has grades, from the forces of inorganic nature through plants and animals to human beings, and these grades are the Platonic Ideas (§25). Because the one will appears in innumerable individuals that compete for the same matter, space, and time, nature is everywhere strife. The clearest image Schopenhauer found is the bulldog ant of Australia, which, when cut in two, sets its head and its tail fighting each other (§27). *On the Will in Nature* (1836) collected confirmations of the doctrine from the natural sciences of his day.

In the second volume Schopenhauer qualified the central claim. Even in self-consciousness the will is known in the form of time, as a succession of acts, so it is not the thing in itself without remainder but its most immediate appearance (WWV II ch. 18). He held fast to the primacy of the will over the intellect, however, and gave it a famous image: the will is the strong blind man who carries on his shoulders the lame man who can see (WWV II ch. 19). The intellect is the will's tool, and what the will does not want known, the intellect does not readily let into consciousness. Nowhere is the will stronger than in sexual love, whose real concern, behind every romance, is nothing less than the composition of the next generation (WWV II ch. 44).

## Life as suffering

All willing springs from lack, and so from suffering. Every satisfaction ends one wish while others remain unfulfilled, and a fulfilled wish soon gives way to a new one (§57). What we call happiness is therefore never given positively.

> Alle Befriedigung, oder was man gemeinhin Glück nennt, ist eigentlich und wesentlich immer nur negativ und durchaus nie positiv.
>
> All satisfaction, or what is commonly called happiness, is really and essentially always only negative and never positive.
>
> (WWV I §58)

When the will finds no object, it falls into an emptiness that is no less painful.

> Sein Leben schwingt also, gleich einem Pendel, hin und her, zwischen dem Schmerz und der Langeweile, welche beide in der Tat dessen letzte Bestandteile sind.
>
> Its life swings like a pendulum, back and forth, between pain and boredom, which are in fact its ultimate constituents.
>
> (WWV I §57)

The suffering is not an accident of circumstance but follows from what the will is, an endless striving that no object can end, and Schopenhauer turned this against the optimism of philosophers and of popular religion alike.

> mir der Optimismus, wo er nicht etwa das gedankenlose Reden solcher ist, unter deren platten Stirnen nichts als Worte herbergen, nicht bloß als eine absurde, sondern auch als eine wahrhaft ruchlose Denkungsart erscheint, als ein bitterer Hohn über die namenlosen Leiden der Menschheit.
>
> optimism, where it is not the thoughtless talk of those under whose flat foreheads nothing but words find lodging, seems to me not merely an absurd but a truly wicked way of thinking, a bitter mockery of the unspeakable sufferings of humanity.
>
> (WWV I §59)

In the second volume he even turned [[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz|Leibniz]]'s claim that this is the best of all possible worlds on its head. The world, he argued, is arranged just well enough to go on existing. Its planetary orbits, its climates, and its species are so precariously balanced that a slightly worse world could not exist at all. Since no world can be worse than one that can barely exist, this one is the worst of all possible worlds (WWV II ch. 46). Whether such passages state an argument or a temperament has been debated ever since. In the 1870s and 1880s the question whether life is worth living divided German philosophy in what came to be called the pessimism controversy.

## Art and the Ideas

Book III describes the one experience in which the circle of willing is broken from within. In aesthetic contemplation we stop considering things according to the principle of sufficient reason, asking where, when, why, and what for, and lose ourselves in the object. The object is then no longer this particular thing but its Idea, and the knower is no longer an individual but a "pure, will-less, painless, timeless subject of knowledge" (§34).

> Es ist der schmerzlose Zustand, den Epikuros als das höchste Gut und als den Zustand der Götter pries: denn wir sind, für jenen Augenblick, des schnöden Willensdranges entledigt, wir feiern den Sabbat der Zuchthausarbeit des Wollens, das Rad des Ixion steht still.
>
> It is the painless state that Epicurus praised as the highest good and as the state of the gods: for that moment we are delivered from the vile pressure of the will, we celebrate the Sabbath of the penal servitude of willing, the wheel of Ixion stands still.
>
> (WWV I §38)

The reference to [[Epicurus]] is apt, since Schopenhauer, like him, can describe happiness only as the absence of pain. Most people can enter this state only briefly. *Genius* is the capacity to remain in it, leaving one's own interests, wishes, and aims entirely out of sight, and Schopenhauer defines it as the most complete objectivity (§36). In the experience of beauty the object invites contemplation; in the *sublime* it threatens the will, and we tear ourselves away from that relation consciously, as when we contemplate a storm at sea from a safe place (§39).

The arts are ranked by the grade of the will's objectification they present. Architecture shows the lowest grades, gravity and rigidity in their conflict; landscape and animal painting, sculpture, and history painting rise through plant, animal, and human forms; poetry presents the Idea of humanity itself, and its summit is tragedy (§§43–51). Tragedy shows the terrible side of life, the triumph of chance and error and the destruction of the just, and its hero atones not for his particular sins but for the guilt of existence itself. Schopenhauer quotes Calderón, *Pues el delito mayor / del hombre es haber nacido*: for the greatest crime of man is that he was born (§51). [[Friedrich Nietzsche|Nietzsche]] would put the same wisdom, in the mouth of Silenus, at the origin of Greek tragedy.

## Music

Every other art, Schopenhauer argues, copies the Ideas and so presents the will only indirectly. Music copies nothing in the world of representation. It could exist even if there were no world, and it stands beside the Ideas as a second and equally direct expression of the will itself.

> Die Musik ist nämlich eine so unmittelbare Objektivation und Abbild des ganzen Willens, wie die Welt selbst es ist, ja wie die Ideen es sind
>
> Music is as immediate an objectification and copy of the whole will as the world itself is, indeed as the Ideas are
>
> (WWV I §52)

[[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz|Leibniz]] had written to Christian Goldbach in 1712 that music is a hidden exercise in arithmetic of a mind that does not know it is counting. Schopenhauer accepts this as a description of music's surface and corrects it at the end of the section: music is *exercitium metaphysices occultum nescientis se philosophari animi*, a hidden exercise in metaphysics of a mind that does not know it is philosophizing (§52). He works the parallel out in detail. The ground bass corresponds to inorganic nature, the lowest grade of the will's objectification; the middle voices of the harmony correspond to the plant and animal worlds; and the melody, with its unbroken connection and its significant intention from beginning to end, corresponds to the reflective life and striving of human beings. Since melody is a constant departure from the keynote and return to it, it reproduces the will's cycle of lack and satisfaction. Music therefore expresses not this or that joy or sorrow, occasioned by particular circumstances, but joy and sorrow themselves, in abstraction and without their motives (§52).

> Demzufolge könnte man die Welt ebenso wohl verkörperte Musik als verkörperten Willen nennen
>
> Accordingly one could call the world embodied music as well as embodied will
>
> (WWV I §52)

In the same section Schopenhauer adds an observation from acoustics. A perfectly pure harmonic system of tones, he says, is impossible "not only physically but even arithmetically." No scale can even be calculated in which every fifth stands to the keynote as 2 : 3, every major third as 4 : 5, and every minor third as 5 : 6, for if the tones are right relative to the keynote, they are no longer right relative to one another; the tones of the scale are like actors who must play now one role, now another. He concludes:

> Daher lässt sich eine vollkommen richtige Musik nicht einmal denken, geschweige ausführen; und deshalb weicht jede mögliche Musik von der vollkommenen Reinheit ab: sie kann bloß ihre wesentlichen Dissonanzen verdecken, durch Verteilung derselben an alle Töne, d. i. durch Temperatur.
>
> Hence a perfectly correct music cannot even be thought, let alone performed, and so every possible music deviates from perfect purity: it can only conceal its essential dissonances by distributing them over all the tones, that is, by temperament.
>
> (WWV I §52)

The remark suits his picture of a will at variance with itself, and it is also correct.

### Remark: the arithmetic of temperament

The [[Pythagoreanism|Pythagoreans]] are said to have discovered that the octave, the fifth, and the fourth correspond to string lengths in the ratios $2 : 1$, $3 : 2$, and $4 : 3$. Write intervals as frequency ratios, so that stacking intervals multiplies them. The pure octave, fifth, and major third are then $2$, $3/2$, and $5/4$, and Schopenhauer's claim becomes a statement about multiplicative relations among rational numbers.

**Proposition.** If integers $a, b, c$ satisfy
$$
2^{a} \left( \tfrac{3}{2} \right)^{b} \left( \tfrac{5}{4} \right)^{c} = 1,
$$
then $a = b = c = 0$. In particular, no nontrivial stack of pure fifths and pure major thirds, up or down, arrives exactly at a pure octave of its starting tone, and $\log_2 (3/2)$ is irrational.

**Proof.** The left-hand side equals $2^{a - b - 2c}\, 3^{b}\, 5^{c}$. By the uniqueness of prime factorization in $\mathbb{Q}^{\times}$, it equals $1$ only if all three exponents vanish, so $b = c = 0$, and then $a = 0$. If $\log_2 (3/2) = p/q$ with $q \ge 1$, then $(3/2)^q = 2^p$, that is, $2^{-p} (3/2)^{q} = 1$ with $q \neq 0$, contradicting what has just been shown. $\blacksquare$

Equivalently, $2$, $3/2$, and $5/4$ generate a free abelian group of rank $3$: every interval built from pure octaves, fifths, and major thirds has a unique address $(a, b, c) \in \mathbb{Z}^3$, a lattice that Euler drew in 1739 and that later music theory calls the *Tonnetz*. The ways in which the lattice fails to close are the classical commas:
$$
\frac{(3/2)^{12}}{2^{7}} = \frac{531441}{524288}, \qquad \frac{(3/2)^{4}}{2^{2} \cdot (5/4)} = \frac{81}{80}, \qquad \frac{2}{(5/4)^{3}} = \frac{128}{125}.
$$
Twelve fifths overshoot seven octaves by the Pythagorean comma; four fifths overshoot two octaves and a major third by the syntonic comma; three major thirds fall short of an octave by the diesis.

Schopenhauer's particular complaint, that tones right with respect to the keynote are wrong with respect to one another, can be seen in the most natural just tuning of the major scale,
$$
1, \quad \tfrac{9}{8}, \quad \tfrac{5}{4}, \quad \tfrac{4}{3}, \quad \tfrac{3}{2}, \quad \tfrac{5}{3}, \quad \tfrac{15}{8}, \quad 2.
$$
Every degree stands in a simple ratio to the keynote. Yet the fifth from the second degree to the sixth is $(5/3)/(9/8) = 40/27$, and the minor third from the second degree to the fourth is $(4/3)/(9/8) = 32/27$; each is smaller than the pure interval, $3/2$ or $6/5$, by exactly the syntonic comma $81/80$. The second degree would have to be $9/8$ to stand a pure fifth above the fifth degree, an octave lower, and $10/9$ to stand a pure fifth below the sixth: it is an actor with two roles.

Temperament distributes the error. Since $\log_2 (3/2)$ is irrational, no division of the octave into $q$ equal steps contains a pure fifth, and the question becomes which $q$ comes closest. The continued fraction expansion
$$
\log_2 \tfrac{3}{2} = 0.5849625\ldots = [0; 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 1, 5, \ldots]
$$
has the convergents $1/1$, $1/2$, $3/5$, $7/12$, $24/41$, $31/53$, $\ldots$, and by a classical theorem going back to Lagrange each convergent $p/q$ approximates $x = \log_2(3/2)$ better, in the sense of $|q x - p|$, than any fraction with a smaller denominator. The convergent $7/12$ is the twelve-tone equal temperament, computed by Zhu Zaiyu in 1584 and by Simon Stevin around 1605. Measured in cents, where an interval with ratio $r$ has $1200 \log_2 r$ cents, its fifth $2^{7/12} \approx 1.4983$ is about $1.96$ cents narrow, exactly a twelfth of the Pythagorean comma; the convergent $31/53$ gives a fifth that is off by less than $0.1$ cent. The distribution is not even, however: the equal-tempered major third $2^{1/3} \approx 1.2599$ is about $13.7$ cents wider than $5/4$. Every possible music, as Schopenhauer said, can only conceal its essential dissonances by distributing them over all the tones. %% link: unique factorization and free abelian groups in Algebra; continued fractions in Number Theory %%

## Character and compassion

Book IV opens by insisting that philosophy can only describe conduct, not prescribe it. An unconditioned "ought" addressed to a will that is free in itself is a contradiction (§53). What there is to describe is character. Every human being has an *empirical* character, the way he acts as observed in time, which follows with strict necessity from an *intelligible* character, his will as it is in itself, outside time. The doctrine is Kant's (KrV A538ff./B566ff.), and Schopenhauer called it, together with the Transcendental Aesthetic, one of the two great diamonds in the crown of Kant's fame (PGM §10). Its consequence is strict determinism in action.

> Du kannst tun, was du willst: aber du kannst, in jedem gegebenen Augenblick deines Lebens, nur ein Bestimmtes wollen und schlechterdings nichts anderes als dieses eine.
>
> You can do what you will; but in any given moment of your life you can will only one definite thing and absolutely nothing other than this one thing.
>
> (PFW II)

Freedom is not abolished but moved. It lies not in what we do, *operari*, but in what we are, *esse*, and our responsibility attaches to our being, of which our deeds are the evidence (PFW V).

In the prize essay on morality Schopenhauer attacked Kant's ethics at its foundation. An absolute "ought" makes sense only as the command of a lawgiver who backs it with reward and punishment, so Kant's imperative form is theological ethics in disguise. And the categorical imperative, applied, asks what I could will as a law when I might be on the receiving end, so that it rests in the end on egoism (PGM §§4–7). What all moralists agree on is only the principle *Neminem laede, imo omnes, quantum potes, iuva*: harm no one, and rather help everyone as much as you can (PGM §6). The question is its foundation. Schopenhauer finds three basic incentives of human action: egoism, which wills one's own good; malice, which wills another's harm; and compassion, which wills another's good (PGM §16). Only actions from the last have moral worth.

> die ganz unmittelbare, von allen anderweitigen Rücksichten unabhängige Teilnahme zunächst am Leiden eines andern
>
> the entirely immediate participation, independent of all other considerations, first of all in the suffering of another
>
> (PGM §16)

From compassion come the two cardinal virtues, justice, which refrains from harm, and loving-kindness, which helps (PGM §§17–18). Compassion also extends to animals. Against Kant, for whom our duties concerning animals are only indirect duties to ourselves, Schopenhauer wrote:

> Mitleid mit Tieren hängt mit der Güte des Charakters so genau zusammen, dass man zuversichtlich behaupten darf, wer gegen Tiere grausam ist, könne kein guter Mensch sein.
>
> Compassion for animals is so closely bound up with goodness of character that one may confidently assert that whoever is cruel to animals cannot be a good person.
>
> (PGM §19)

That we should feel another's suffering as our own is, on the face of it, a mystery, and Schopenhauer called it the great mystery of ethics (PGM §16). His metaphysics resolves it. If the will is one, and plurality belongs only to its appearances, then the compassionate person sees through the *principium individuationis* and recognizes in the other the same being that he is himself. Schopenhauer finds this expressed in the formula of the Upanishads, *tat tvam asi*, "that art thou" (§63). The same insight grounds what he calls eternal justice. Since tormentor and tormented are one will, the will always bears in its own suffering what it inflicts in its appearances: "the tormentor and the tormented are one" (§63). Love in the ethical sense is nothing else: "all true and pure love is compassion, and every love that is not compassion is selfishness" (§66).

## The denial of the will

The knowledge that sees through the *principium individuationis* can go further than compassion. Whoever recognizes his own inner nature in all things also takes the suffering of the whole world as his own. For such a person the will to life can turn. Knowledge, which until then had served the will as its tool, becomes a quieter (*Quietiv*) of all willing, and the will, no longer affirming its own nature as it appears in the body, denies it (§68). The outward signs are those of the saints of every religion: voluntary chastity, voluntary poverty, fasting, and the patient acceptance of injury. Schopenhauer found them in the Christian mystics, Meister Eckhart and Madame Guyon among them, in the Hindu renouncers, and in Buddhist monks, and of all religions he judged Buddhism the closest to the result of his philosophy (WWV II ch. 17). The transformation cannot be achieved by resolution. It comes suddenly, as if from outside, and Schopenhauer compared it to what the Church calls the effect of grace (§70).

This denial is not self-destruction. Suicide, Schopenhauer argued, is far from a denial of the will; it is an emphatic affirmation of it, since the person who ends his life wills life and rejects only the conditions under which it has been given (§69). What denial leaves behind can be described only negatively. Schopenhauer borrows the word for it from Kant's table of the concept of nothing (KrV A290/B346): it is a relative nothing, *nihil privativum*, the absence of everything we know, not an absolute nothing, *nihil negativum* (§71). The first volume ends:

> Wir bekennen es vielmehr frei: was nach gänzlicher Aufhebung des Willens übrig bleibt, ist für alle die, welche noch des Willens voll sind, allerdings nichts. Aber auch umgekehrt ist denen, in welchen der Wille sich gewendet und verneint hat, diese unsere so sehr reale Welt mit allen ihren Sonnen und Milchstraßen – nichts.
>
> We freely acknowledge it: what remains after the complete abolition of the will is, for all who are still full of will, indeed nothing. But conversely, to those in whom the will has turned and denied itself, this very real world of ours, with all its suns and Milky Ways, is nothing.
>
> (WWV I §71)

## After Schopenhauer

- After Oxenford's article Schopenhauer became, in the last decade of his life, the most widely read philosopher in Germany. The failure of the revolutions of 1848 had prepared an audience for a philosophy that expected nothing from history.
- [[Friedrich Nietzsche|Nietzsche]] found the main work by chance in a Leipzig bookshop in 1865. *The Birth of Tragedy* (1872) is built on Schopenhauer's metaphysics of music, and *Schopenhauer as Educator* (1874) celebrates him as a model of the philosopher. Nietzsche later replaced the will to life with the will to power, read the denial of the will as a symptom of exhausted life, and turned the "pure, will-less, painless, timeless subject of knowledge" against its author (GM III §12).
- Richard Wagner read the book in 1854, at the suggestion of the poet Georg Herwegh, and called it a gift from heaven. *Tristan und Isolde* (1857–1859) is its most famous musical consequence. Wagner sent Schopenhauer the libretto of the *Ring* "out of reverence and gratitude"; Schopenhauer, who preferred Rossini, was unimpressed.
- Eduard von Hartmann's *Philosophy of the Unconscious* (1869) combined Schopenhauer's will with Hegel's reason and became the occasion of the pessimism controversy. In literature, Tolstoy, Thomas Hardy, Proust, Thomas Mann, whose *Buddenbrooks* (1901) gives a merchant near the end of his life a chapter of the main work to read, Samuel Beckett, and Jorge Luis Borges read him closely.
- [[Sigmund Freud|Freud]] learned from Otto Rank that the chapter on madness (WWV II ch. 32) had anticipated repression, and he wrote that the large extent to which psychoanalysis coincides with Schopenhauer's philosophy, in the dominance of the affects, the importance of sexuality, and the mechanism of repression, was not due to acquaintance with it, since he had read Schopenhauer very late (*An Autobiographical Study*, 1925).
- [[Ludwig Wittgenstein|Wittgenstein]] read the main work as a young man, and its traces are visible in the *Tractatus*: the metaphysical subject that is not in the world but a limit of it, like the eye that does not see itself (5.632–5.633), and the remark that the world of the happy is a different world from that of the unhappy (6.43).
- Albert Einstein wrote in "The World as I See It" (1931) that Schopenhauer's saying, in the compact form "a man can do what he wills, but he cannot will what he wills," had been a consolation to him since his youth. The saying is a paraphrase of the passage in the essay on freedom quoted above.
- [[Max Scheler|Scheler]]'s *The Nature of Sympathy* (1913; 2nd ed. 1923) criticized Schopenhauer's account of compassion as a metaphysical identification that loses the distinctness of the other person. In the Frankfurt School, Max Horkheimer turned to Schopenhauer in his later years.
- Schopenhauer's insistence that animals are owed compassion made him a forerunner of later animal ethics. His essay "On Women" in the *Parerga*, by contrast, is now read mainly as a document of misogyny.

## Reading order

Schopenhauer insisted that *On the Fourfold Root*, preferably in its second edition of 1847, be read first, and it is short. In the first volume of the main work, a good path is Book I (§§1–7, 15), Book II (§§17–19, 21–24, 27), Book III (§§33–39, 51–52), and Book IV (§§53–59, 63, 66, 68–71). The "Criticism of the Kantian Philosophy" follows naturally, and of the second volume, chapters 17, 18, 19, 44, and 46 add the most. *On the Basis of Morality* is the clearest statement of the ethics. The "Aphorisms on the Wisdom of Life" in the *Parerga*, his most popular work, deliberately set the metaphysics aside to give advice on living as well as one can, and are best read last.
