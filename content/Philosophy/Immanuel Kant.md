Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) is where the two streams of early modern philosophy in this series meet. The rationalists, from [[René Descartes|Descartes]] to [[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz|Leibniz]], held that reason alone could reach the deepest truths about reality; the empiricists, ending with [[David Hume|Hume]], showed how little can be established once reason is confined to what the senses supply. Kant accepted the strongest point of each and drew a boundary around what reason can know. Much of what follows in this series is a response to where he drew it. [[Fichte and Schelling|Fichte, Schelling]], and [[Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel|Hegel]] tried to erase it, [[Arthur Schopenhauer|Schopenhauer]] built on it, [[Friedrich Nietzsche|Nietzsche]] mocked it as the "Königsbergian" stage of the true world, and [[Edmund Husserl|Husserl]] and [[Martin Heidegger|Heidegger]] returned to it with questions of their own.

The thread this post follows is the one Kant named in the title of his main work: a *critique* of reason, an examination that determines what reason can and cannot do. It has three parts. Reason can know some things about the world independently of experience, because the world as we experience it must conform to the forms of our own cognition; this is the Copernican revolution. It can know them only within the bounds of possible experience, and beyond those bounds it falls into illusions it cannot avoid; this is the Transcendental Dialectic. And what theoretical reason cannot know, practical reason must nonetheless presuppose, above all freedom; this is the moral philosophy. Kant compressed the whole movement into one sentence:

> Ich musste also das Wissen aufheben, um zum Glauben Platz zu bekommen.
>
> So I had to put knowledge aside in order to make room for faith.
>
> (KrV Bxxx)

*Glaube* here does not mean faith in revelation. It is the rational faith in freedom, immortality, and God that the moral philosophy defends, discussed below.

## Life

Kant was born on 22 April 1724 in Königsberg, East Prussia, the son of a harness maker, and grew up in a Pietist household. He entered the university of Königsberg in 1740, spent several years as a private tutor on estates in the East Prussian countryside, and returned in 1755 as a *Privatdozent*, an unsalaried lecturer paid by student fees. For fifteen years he lectured on logic, metaphysics, ethics, mathematics, physics, and physical geography. He never travelled far from his home city. His work is customarily divided into three periods.

- **Precritical (to 1770).** Work in natural philosophy and in the rationalist metaphysics of the Leibniz–Wolff school. *Universal Natural History and Theory of the Heavens* (1755) proposed that the solar system formed from a rotating cloud of matter, the nebular hypothesis later developed independently by Laplace.
- **The silent decade (1770–1781).** Appointed professor of logic and metaphysics in 1770, Kant defended an Inaugural Dissertation that already treats space and time as forms of sensibility. He then published almost nothing for eleven years while he worked out the *Critique*.
- **Critical (1781–1798).** The three *Critiques*, the *Prolegomena*, the *Groundwork*, and the writings on religion, politics, and history. *Religion within the Bounds of Mere Reason* (1793) drew a royal rebuke in 1794, and Kant promised not to write publicly on religion again; after the king died in 1797, he considered himself released from the promise.

He died on 12 February 1804.

Two influences matter for the philosophy. One is Hume, discussed in the next section. The other is [[Jean-Jacques Rousseau|Rousseau]]. In a private note Kant recalls a time when he believed that knowledge alone made up the honor of humanity and despised the common people who knew nothing; Rousseau, he writes, set him right, and he learned to honor human beings (AA 20:44). A portrait of Rousseau is said to have been the only picture in his house. The conviction that a person's worth does not depend on learning returns in the moral philosophy, in the claim that the only thing good without limitation is a good will.

## The problem: Hume and the dogmatic slumber

The metaphysics Kant was trained in, that of Christian Wolff and ultimately of Leibniz, claimed to prove from pure concepts the existence of God, the immortality of the soul, and the basic structure of the world. Hume had attacked the foundation of such claims. Our belief that every event has a cause, he argued, can be justified neither by reason, since there is no contradiction in an event without a cause, nor by experience, since experience shows only constant conjunction and never necessary connection. Necessity is a habit of the mind, projected onto things.

> Ich gestehe frei: die Erinnerung des David Hume war eben dasjenige, was mir vor vielen Jahren zuerst den dogmatischen Schlummer unterbrach
>
> I openly confess that it was David Hume's warning that, many years ago, first broke my dogmatic slumber
>
> (Prol 4:260)

Kant's first step was to generalize the problem. The connection of cause and effect, he found, is far from the only concept through which the understanding thinks connections between things a priori; metaphysics consists of nothing else (Prol 4:260). If such concepts can come neither from experience nor from logic, then metaphysics as a whole is in question. Kant even suggests that had Hume posed the problem in full generality, he would have seen that it threatened pure mathematics too, and his good sense would have kept him from that conclusion (B19–20). Either there is a third source of necessary knowledge, or metaphysics, and much else, is impossible.

## Analytic, synthetic, a priori

The *Critique* begins by conceding half of empiricism.

> Dass alle unsere Erkenntnis mit der Erfahrung anfange, daran ist gar kein Zweifel … Wenn aber gleich alle unsere Erkenntnis mit der Erfahrung anhebt, so entspringt sie darum doch nicht eben alle aus der Erfahrung.
>
> That all our cognition starts with experience, there is no doubt at all … Yet even though all our cognition starts with experience, it does not follow that it all springs from experience.
>
> (KrV B1)

To make the question precise, Kant crosses two distinctions. A judgment is *a priori* if its justification is independent of all experience, and *a posteriori* if it depends on experience. A judgment is *analytic* if its predicate is already contained, covertly, in the concept of its subject, so that denying it yields a contradiction, and *synthetic* if the predicate adds something the subject concept does not contain (A6–7/B10–11).

| | Analytic | Synthetic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A priori** | "All bodies are extended." | "7 + 5 = 12"; "Every event has a cause." |
| **A posteriori** | (none) | "All bodies are heavy." |

The bottom-left cell is empty because it would be absurd to ground an analytic judgment on experience: to know it, I need not go beyond my concept at all (B11). The interesting cell is the top right. Kant argues that mathematics lives there. The concept of the sum of 7 and 5 contains only the union of the two numbers into one, not which number that one is. To reach 12 we have to go beyond the concepts and call on intuition, five fingers say, adding the units of the 5 one by one to the 7 (B15). Likewise, "the straight line between two points is the shortest" is synthetic, because the concept *straight* contains nothing about magnitude, only a quality (B16). Metaphysics, if it is possible at all, would also have to consist of synthetic judgments a priori. The whole *Critique* is organized around one question:

> Wie sind synthetische Urteile a priori möglich?
>
> How are synthetic judgments a priori possible?
>
> (KrV B19)

## The Copernican revolution

Kant's answer begins with a reversal.

> Bisher nahm man an, alle unsere Erkenntnis müsse sich nach den Gegenständen richten; … Man versuche es daher einmal, ob wir nicht in den Aufgaben der Metaphysik damit besser fortkommen, dass wir annehmen, die Gegenstände müssen sich nach unserem Erkenntnis richten
>
> Up to now it was assumed that all our cognition must be governed by objects; … So let us try, for once, whether we fare better with the tasks of metaphysics if we assume that objects must be governed by our cognition
>
> (KrV Bxvi)

Copernicus, unable to explain the motions of the heavens on the assumption that the stars revolve around the observer, tried whether he might do better by letting the observer revolve and the stars stand still. Kant proposes the same move for knowledge. If our cognition had to conform to objects as they are in themselves, a priori knowledge of them would be inexplicable: how could I know in advance what an independent thing must be like? But if objects, as they appear to us, must conform to the conditions under which anything can appear to us at all, then I can know those conditions a priori, and with them a priori truths about every possible object of experience. Kant calls an inquiry of this kind *transcendental*: it is concerned not with objects but with our way of knowing objects, insofar as that is possible a priori (B25).

The reversal does not mean that the mind creates its objects. The matter of experience must be given to us; only an intuitive intellect, which Kant ascribes at most to God, could produce its objects by thinking them (B72, B145). What the mind contributes is form: the ways in which anything given must be ordered in order to be experienced, and the concepts through which it must be thought in order to be an object at all.

## Sensibility and understanding

Kant distinguishes two sources of cognition. *Sensibility* is the capacity to receive representations, and its products are *intuitions*: immediate, singular representations of objects. *Understanding* is the capacity to think, and its products are *concepts*: general representations that can apply to many things. Neither can do the other's work.

> Gedanken ohne Inhalt sind leer, Anschauungen ohne Begriffe sind blind.
>
> Thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind.
>
> (KrV A51/B75)

**Space and time** (the Transcendental Aesthetic). Space and time, Kant argues, are neither things nor relations between things in themselves but the forms of our sensibility: space is the form of outer sense, time the form of inner sense. This is what makes mathematics possible as synthetic a priori knowledge. Mathematical cognition, unlike philosophical cognition, proceeds by the *construction* of concepts (A713/B741): the geometer draws the triangle, in imagination or on paper, and reads off from the figure what the concept alone does not contain. Geometry describes the form of outer intuition, and arithmetic produces its numbers by adding units successively in time (Prol §10). Space and time are therefore *empirically real*, valid without exception for everything we can ever encounter, and *transcendentally ideal*, nothing at all once we abstract from the conditions of our intuition (A28/B44, A35–36/B52).

**The categories** (the Transcendental Analytic). To think is to judge, and from the logical forms of judgment Kant derives twelve pure concepts of the understanding, the *categories*, under which every object must fall in order to be thought as an object (A80/B106).

| Quantity | Quality | Relation | Modality |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Unity | Reality | Substance and accident | Possibility and impossibility |
| Plurality | Negation | Cause and effect | Existence and nonexistence |
| Totality | Limitation | Community | Necessity and contingency |

The hardest step in the book, the Transcendental Deduction, argues that the categories are not merely forms of our thinking that the world might fail to fit, but conditions of there being an experienced world at all. Its pivot is the unity of self-consciousness.

> Das: Ich denke, muss alle meine Vorstellungen begleiten können
>
> The "I think" must be able to accompany all my representations
>
> (KrV B131)

Otherwise something would be represented in me that could not be thought at all, which is to say that the representation would be either impossible or at least nothing to me (B131–132). Representations count as mine only if they can be combined in one consciousness, and combining them is precisely what the categories do. So whatever I can be conscious of as an object must already conform to them.

**Kant's answer to Hume** (the Second Analogy). When I look over a house, I can take in its parts in any order; when I watch a ship drift downstream, the order of my perceptions is fixed by the event itself (A190–193/B235–238). I can tell an objective succession from a merely subjective one only if I think the later state as following the earlier according to a rule, that is, as caused. Causality is therefore not, as Hume thought, a habit added to an experience that is already complete. It is a condition of experiencing an objective order in time in the first place. Kant concedes to Hume that the concept of cause cannot be derived from experience; he denies that it is therefore a mere habit.

### Remark: Kant's mathematics after Kant

Kant's examples from arithmetic and geometry have both been reexamined with tools he did not have.

**Arithmetic.** Work in the language with a constant $0$, a function symbol $S$ for successor, and $+$. Take the two defining equations of addition,
$$
x + 0 = x, \qquad x + S(y) = S(x + y),
$$
and write $\overline{n}$ for the numeral $S^n(0)$. Then
$$
\overline{7} + \overline{5} = S(\overline{7} + \overline{4}) = S^2(\overline{7} + \overline{3}) = \cdots = S^5(\overline{7} + 0) = S^5(\overline{7}) = \overline{12},
$$
using the second equation five times and the first once. No induction is needed: Robinson arithmetic $\mathsf{Q}$, which has these equations but no induction schema, proves every true equation between closed terms. Derivations of this kind underlie [[Gottlob Frege|Frege]]'s case against Kant. In the *Grundlagen der Arithmetik* (1884) he held that arithmetical truths are analytic, provable from logical laws and definitions alone, and he pointed out that [[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz|Leibniz]]'s earlier proof of $2 + 2 = 4$ had a gap: it silently assumed the associativity of addition (§6).

General laws are another matter. $\mathsf{Q}$ does not prove $\forall x\, \forall y\, (x + y = y + x)$; commutativity needs induction. Poincaré (*La Science et l'Hypothèse*, 1902) argued that the principle of induction, which can be established neither by analytic proof nor by experiment, is the very type of a synthetic a priori judgment. That relocates Kant's thesis from individual sums to the principle that governs all of them. And by Gödel's incompleteness theorem (1931), in the form Rosser gave it (1936), no consistent, recursively axiomatized theory extending $\mathsf{Q}$ is complete, so arithmetical truth outruns every fixed system of derivations. Whether this vindicates anything specifically Kantian is disputed; see [[Kurt Gödel]] and [[Philosophy of Mathematics]]. %% link: Robinson arithmetic in Logic %%

**Geometry.** In the Postulates of Empirical Thought, Kant gives an example of a concept that is consistent and yet impossible:

> So ist in dem Begriffe einer Figur, die in zwei geraden Linien eingeschlossen ist, kein Widerspruch
>
> Thus the concept of a figure bounded by two straight lines contains no contradiction
>
> (KrV A220/B268)

The impossibility, he says, lies not in the concept but in its construction in space. In the Euclidean plane two distinct lines meet at most once, so they cannot bound a region. Read "straight line" as "geodesic", though, and the figure can be constructed. On the unit sphere $S^2$, any two distinct great circles meet in a pair of antipodal points $p$ and $-p$ and divide the sphere into four lunes, each bounded by two geodesic arcs from $p$ to $-p$. Kant's other geometric example, that the straight line is the shortest path between two points, fares the same way. On a Riemannian manifold, geodesics are only *locally* length-minimizing: two non-antipodal points of $S^2$ are joined by two arcs of the great circle through them, both geodesics, and only the shorter is a shortest path. %% link: geodesics in Differential Geometry %%

Kant was right that such concepts involve no contradiction, and the way to show it is to exhibit a model. That is how non-Euclidean geometry was legitimized: Beltrami (1868) and Klein built models of hyperbolic geometry inside Euclidean geometry, so that hyperbolic geometry is consistent if Euclidean geometry is. What Kant did not anticipate is that the geometry of physical space would become an empirical question, raised by Riemann in his habilitation lecture of 1854 and given a definite physical answer by general relativity (1915), in which spacetime is curved. Kant's defenders reply that his claim concerned the form of human intuition, not physical space. Whether that form must be Euclidean is a further question; Helmholtz argued in 1870 that we can imagine what experience in a non-Euclidean space would be like.

## Phenomena and things in themselves

The price of the Copernican revolution is a limit. If what we know a priori are the conditions under which things can appear to us, then we know things only as they appear, as *phenomena*, never as they are in themselves. The categories have meaning only when applied to something that can be given in intuition. Used beyond any possible intuition, they are empty forms of thought. The concept of a *noumenon*, a thing thought by the understanding alone, is legitimate only in a negative sense, as the concept of something that is not an object of our sensible intuition (B307). It marks a boundary rather than describing what lies beyond it.

Kant calls the resulting position *transcendental idealism* and insists that it is at the same time an *empirical realism*: within experience, tables and planets are perfectly real, not mere ideas in anyone's head. Two families of interpretation have formed around this combination. On the *two-world* reading, appearances and things in themselves are two sets of entities, and appearances are something like representations. On the *two-aspect* reading, associated above all with Henry Allison (*Kant's Transcendental Idealism*, 1983), there is one set of things, considered in two ways: as they appear under the conditions of our cognition, and as they may be in abstraction from those conditions.

Either way, the thing in itself became the system's most criticized concept. Friedrich Heinrich Jacobi complained in 1787 that without the assumption of things in themselves he could not enter Kant's system, and with it he could not remain inside. Kant says that things in themselves affect our sensibility, yet causality is a category valid only for appearances. The German idealists, beginning with [[Fichte and Schelling|Fichte]], took the objection as decisive and gave up the thing in itself, and with it Kant's boundary.

## The illusions of reason

The first preface opens with a sentence that summarizes the second half of the *Critique*:

> Die menschliche Vernunft hat das besondere Schicksal in einer Gattung ihrer Erkenntnisse: dass sie durch Fragen belästigt wird, die sie nicht abweisen kann; denn sie sind ihr durch die Natur der Vernunft selbst aufgegeben, die sie aber auch nicht beantworten kann; denn sie übersteigen alles Vermögen der menschlichen Vernunft.
>
> In one kind of its cognitions, human reason has a peculiar fate: it is troubled by questions it cannot turn away, because reason's own nature sets them, and yet cannot answer, because they surpass all its powers.
>
> (KrV Avii)

Reason, in the narrow sense of the Transcendental Dialectic, is the demand to find for every conditioned cognition of the understanding the unconditioned that completes it (A307/B364). Applied to the thinking subject, to the series of conditions in the world, and to the ground of everything, the demand produces three *ideas*: the soul, the world as a whole, and God. Each corresponds to a branch of the old metaphysics, and each produces its own kind of illusion.

- **The soul** (the Paralogisms). Rational psychology infers from the "I think" that the self is a simple, persisting substance. The inference trades on an ambiguity. The "I" of apperception is a formal condition of all thought, not an object given in intuition, so the category of substance cannot be applied to it.
- **The world** (the Antinomies). Rational cosmology produces pairs of contrary claims, each of which can be proved by refuting the other.
- **God** (the Ideal). Rational theology claims to prove the existence of a most real being. Kant argues that there are only three possible proofs, the ontological, the cosmological, and the physico-theological, that the last two rest on the first, and that the first fails.

The four antinomies (A426–461/B454–489) are the most striking.

|     | Thesis                                                                               | Antithesis                                                                            |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | The world has a beginning in time and is bounded in space.                           | The world has no beginning and no bounds; it is infinite in time and in space.        |
| 2   | Every composite substance in the world consists of simple parts.                     | No composite thing consists of simple parts; nothing simple exists anywhere.          |
| 3   | Besides causality according to laws of nature, there is a causality through freedom. | There is no freedom; everything happens according to laws of nature alone.            |
| 4   | There belongs to the world, as its part or its cause, an absolutely necessary being. | No absolutely necessary being exists, either in the world or outside it as its cause. |

Both sides of each antinomy assume that the world is a thing in itself, given as a complete whole. Kant's resolution drops the assumption. In the first two, the *mathematical* antinomies, both sides are then false: the world of appearances is never given as a whole, finite or infinite, but only as a regress of conditions that is set as a task and can always be continued. In the last two, the *dynamical* antinomies, both sides may be true, the antithesis of appearances and the thesis of things in themselves. Every event in nature has a natural cause, and yet the same action, considered as the deed of a thing in itself, may be free. Kant does not claim to have proved that we are free, only that freedom does not contradict natural necessity. He also presents the resolution as an indirect proof of transcendental idealism: the antinomies arise if appearances are things in themselves and vanish if they are not (A506/B534).

The critique of the ontological argument, the argument of [[Anselm of Canterbury|Anselm]] and [[René Descartes|Descartes]] that God's existence follows from the concept of a most perfect being, contains Kant's most quoted logical point.

> Sein ist offenbar kein reales Prädikat, d. i. ein Begriff von irgend etwas, was zu dem Begriffe eines Dinges hinzukommen könne.
>
> Being is plainly not a real predicate, that is, not a concept of anything that could be added to the concept of a thing.
>
> (KrV A598/B626)

Existence, Kant says, is the positing of a thing together with all its predicates, not one more predicate among them; a hundred actual thalers contain not a cent more than a hundred possible ones (A599/B627). [[Gottlob Frege|Frege]] made the point precise. Existence is a property not of objects but of concepts: to say that there are Fs is to deny that the number of Fs is zero, and for this reason, Frege notes, the ontological argument breaks down (*Grundlagen*, §53). In first-order logic, existence is expressed by the quantifier, as in $\exists x\, F(x)$, not by a predicate of individuals.

The ideas of reason are not useless. Used *constitutively*, as if they described objects, they produce illusion. Used *regulatively*, as directions for inquiry, they are indispensable: the idea of a systematic unity of nature tells science to look for ever more general laws, without claiming that the unity has been found.

## Practical reason: freedom and the moral law

The *Groundwork* begins with a claim about value.

> Es ist überall nichts in der Welt, ja überhaupt auch außer derselben zu denken möglich, was ohne Einschränkung für gut könnte gehalten werden, als allein ein guter Wille.
>
> Nothing can possibly be thought, anywhere in the world or even outside it, that could be held good without qualification except a good will.
>
> (GMS 4:393)

Talents, fortune, even happiness can be bad when the will that uses them is bad. A good will is good not because of what it accomplishes but through its willing alone (GMS 4:394): it acts from duty, out of respect for the moral law, and not merely from inclination.

The moral law commands categorically. A *hypothetical* imperative says what to do if one wants a certain end, and binds only those who want it. A *categorical* imperative binds unconditionally, whatever one wants (GMS 4:414). Kant gives the categorical imperative several formulations, of which three are central.

> Handle nur nach derjenigen Maxime, durch die du zugleich wollen kannst, dass sie ein allgemeines Gesetz werde.
>
> Act only by that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law.
>
> (GMS 4:421)

> Handle so, dass du die Menschheit, sowohl in deiner Person, als in der Person eines jeden andern, jederzeit zugleich als Zweck, niemals bloß als Mittel brauchst.
>
> Act so that you treat humanity, in your own person as well as in the person of every other, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means.
>
> (GMS 4:429)

The third is the idea of *autonomy*: the will of every rational being as a will that gives universal law, and the moral community as a *kingdom of ends*, in which each member is both legislator and subject (GMS 4:431–433). The first formula states the *form* of moral maxims, universality; the second their *matter*, rational nature as an end in itself; the third combines the two (GMS 4:436).

Kant's standard example is the lying promise. Someone in need considers borrowing money with a promise he knows he cannot keep. Universalized, the maxim destroys itself: if everyone made false promises when in need, no one would believe a promise, and the maxim could not even be acted on (GMS 4:422). Judged by the second formula, the same act uses the lender merely as a means, since the lender could not possibly agree to it (GMS 4:429–430).

Freedom is the hinge between the two halves of the critical system. According to the *Critique of Practical Reason*, we are conscious of the moral law immediately, as a "fact of reason" (KpV 5:31). The moral law is the ground by which we know that we are free (*ratio cognoscendi*), while freedom is the ground of the moral law's existence (*ratio essendi*) (KpV 5:4n). What the third antinomy left merely possible, practical reason entitles us to assume. Practical reason further postulates the immortality of the soul and the existence of God, as conditions under which the highest good, happiness in proportion to virtue, can be hoped for. These are the objects of the faith for which knowledge had to make room.

The book's conclusion opens with its most famous sentence:

> Zwei Dinge erfüllen das Gemüt mit immer neuer und zunehmender Bewunderung und Ehrfurcht, je öfter und anhaltender sich das Nachdenken damit beschäftigt: der bestirnte Himmel über mir, und das moralische Gesetz in mir.
>
> Two things fill the mind with admiration and awe that are always new and always growing, the more often and the longer thought dwells on them: the starry sky above me and the moral law within me.
>
> (KpV 5:161)

## Judgment: beauty and purpose

Between the domain of nature, governed by the categories, and the domain of freedom, governed by the moral law, Kant still saw an immense gulf (KU 5:175–176). The third *Critique* tries to bridge it through the power of *judgment*, the capacity to think the particular as contained under the universal. When the universal is given, judgment is *determining*; when only the particular is given and a universal must be found for it, judgment is *reflecting* (KU 5:179). Aesthetic and teleological judgments are both reflecting.

A judgment of taste, "this is beautiful," is analyzed under the four headings of the table of categories.

- **Quality** (§§1–5). It is disinterested: the pleasure does not depend on any desire for the object's existence.
- **Quantity** (§§6–9). It claims everyone's agreement, yet not on the basis of a concept; no proof can compel anyone to find a rose beautiful.
- **Relation** (§§10–17). It finds in the object a purposive form without the representation of a purpose.
- **Modality** (§§18–22). It claims necessary assent, on the presupposition of a common sense shared by all judging subjects.

> Schönheit ist Form der Zweckmäßigkeit eines Gegenstandes, sofern sie ohne Vorstellung eines Zwecks an ihm wahrgenommen wird.
>
> Beauty is an object's form of purposiveness, so far as this is perceived in it without the representation of any purpose.
>
> (KU §17)

The *sublime* (§§23–29) is the experience of something that overwhelms the imagination, by its size in the mathematically sublime or by its power in the dynamically sublime, and thereby makes us aware of a faculty in ourselves, reason, that exceeds every measure of sense.

Teleological judgment concerns organisms, natural things whose parts are reciprocally cause and effect of one another. Kant held that we can understand them only by judging them as if they were designed, and that it would be absurd to hope for a Newton who could one day make even a blade of grass intelligible through natural laws not arranged by any design (KU §75). Darwin's theory, published nearly seventy years later, is often read as an answer to that challenge.

## Enlightenment

Kant's best-known short essay answers a question raised in the *Berlinische Monatsschrift* in 1784.

> Aufklärung ist der Ausgang des Menschen aus seiner selbstverschuldeten Unmündigkeit.
>
> Enlightenment is the human being's exit from an immaturity of its own making.
>
> (WA 8:35)

Immaturity is the inability to use one's own understanding without another's guidance, and it is self-incurred when its cause lies not in a lack of understanding but in a lack of resolve and courage. The motto of enlightenment is therefore *Sapere aude*: have the courage to use your own understanding. Kant locates freedom of thought in the *public* use of reason, a scholar addressing the reading public, which must always be free, as opposed to the *private* use of reason in an office or post, which may be restricted (WA 8:37). In 1984 [[Michel Foucault|Foucault]] wrote an essay with the same title, reading Kant's essay as the beginning of a philosophy that asks what our own present is.

## After Kant

- [[Fichte and Schelling|Fichte, Schelling]], and [[Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel|Hegel]] dropped the thing in itself and tried to derive the forms of experience from a single principle, turning critique into a system of absolute idealism. The intellectual intuition that Kant denied to human beings became central for Fichte and Schelling.
- [[Arthur Schopenhauer|Schopenhauer]] considered the distinction between appearance and thing in itself Kant's greatest merit, identified the thing in itself with the will, and appended a long critique of the Kantian philosophy to *The World as Will and Representation*.
- Neo-Kantianism began with Otto Liebmann's *Kant und die Epigonen* (1865), each of whose chapters ends with a call to go back to Kant. Its Marburg school (Cohen, Natorp, Cassirer) read Kant above all as a theorist of scientific knowledge.
- [[Edmund Husserl|Husserl]]'s phenomenology took its own transcendental turn with the *Ideas* of 1913. [[Martin Heidegger|Heidegger]]'s *Kant and the Problem of Metaphysics* (1929), and his debate with Cassirer at Davos the same year, read the first *Critique* as a laying of the foundations of ontology rather than a theory of knowledge.
- [[Friedrich Nietzsche|Nietzsche]] placed Kant at the third stage of the history of the "true world," the idea grown pale, Nordic, Königsbergian, and called the thing in itself a contradiction in terms.
- [[Gottlob Frege|Frege]] agreed that geometry is synthetic a priori but argued that arithmetic is analytic. [[Philosophy of Mathematics#Intuitionism|Brouwer's intuitionism]] gave up the apriority of space and held all the more firmly to that of time, and [[Philosophy of Mathematics#Formalism|Hilbert]] opened his *Foundations of Geometry* (1899) with a sentence from the *Critique*.
- The logical empiricists of the [[Vienna Circle]] denied that there are synthetic judgments a priori at all: mathematics is analytic, and geometry, applied to physical space, is empirical.
- In ethics, Kantian moral theory remains one of the main positions alongside consequentialism and virtue ethics, and John Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* (1971) offered a Kantian interpretation of its principles of justice.

## Reading order

The *Prolegomena*, Kant's own short presentation of the theoretical philosophy, is the best first step. In the *Critique of Pure Reason*, the B preface and introduction, the Transcendental Aesthetic, the Second Analogy, and the Antinomies give the shape of the whole before one attempts the Transcendental Deduction. The *Groundwork* comes before the *Critique of Practical Reason*. In the *Critique of the Power of Judgment*, §§1–29, on the beautiful and the sublime, can be read on their own. The essay on enlightenment can be read at any point.
