Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel (1770–1831) is where the line of German idealism in this series reaches its end. [[Immanuel Kant|Kant]] drew a boundary around what reason can know, and [[Fichte and Schelling]] began to erase it. Hegel built the system in which no boundary of that kind remains. Almost everyone in the rest of the series takes a position on him: [[Ludwig Feuerbach|Feuerbach]] and [[Karl Marx|Marx]] by setting his philosophy on its feet, [[Søren Kierkegaard|Kierkegaard]] by insisting on the existing individual whom the system leaves out, [[Arthur Schopenhauer|Schopenhauer]] by contempt, and in the twentieth century [[Martin Heidegger|Heidegger]], [[Jean-Paul Sartre|Sartre]], [[Jacques Lacan|Lacan]], [[Gilles Deleuze|Deleuze]], and [[Jacques Derrida|Derrida]]. Analytic philosophy, too, began as a revolt against Hegelianism, when [[Bertrand Russell|Russell]] and G. E. Moore turned against the British idealism of their teachers.

This post follows the claim on which Hegel staked the system: reason is not confined to appearances, because the limits Kant found in it are reason's own work and can be comprehended by it. The claim is worked out in three steps. The *Phenomenology of Spirit* argues that consciousness, testing its own standards, is driven from the simplest certainty about the world to *absolute knowing*, a standpoint at which the opposition between thought and its object no longer holds. The *Science of Logic* then develops, from that standpoint, the pure determinations of thought, beginning with being and nothing, by a method in which each concept turns into its opposite. And the philosophy of nature and spirit finds the same rational structure in the world, above all in history, which Hegel called the progress of the consciousness of freedom. The Preface to the *Phenomenology* compresses the program into a few lines:

> Das Wahre ist das Ganze. Das Ganze aber ist nur das durch seine Entwicklung sich vollendende Wesen. Es ist von dem Absoluten zu sagen, dass es wesentlich Resultat, dass es erst am Ende das ist, was es in Wahrheit ist.
>
> The true is the whole. But the whole is only the essence that completes itself through its development. Of the absolute it must be said that it is essentially a result, that only at the end is it what it truly is.
>
> (PhG ¶20)

## Life

Hegel was born on 27 August 1770 in Stuttgart, the capital of the Duchy of Württemberg, where his father was a secretary in the ducal revenue office. In 1788 he entered the Tübingen Stift, the Protestant seminary that trained Württemberg's pastors and teachers, and there he shared a room with two younger students who would become famous before he did: the poet Friedrich Hölderlin and Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling. The three read Kant, [[Jean-Jacques Rousseau|Rousseau]], and [[Baruch Spinoza|Spinoza]] and followed the French Revolution with enthusiasm. Hölderlin wrote into Hegel's album the Greek formula *hen kai pan*, "one and all," which Jacobi had reported as the creed of the Spinozist Lessing. After his theological examination in 1793, Hegel worked as a private tutor to patrician families in Bern (1793–1796) and, through Hölderlin, in Frankfurt (1797–1800). A single sheet in his hand from 1796 or 1797, now known as the "Earliest System-Programme of German Idealism," calls for a "mythology of reason"; whether Hegel, Schelling, or Hölderlin composed it is still disputed.

In 1801 a small inheritance allowed him to join Schelling, already a professor, at the University of Jena. His first philosophical book, the *Differenzschrift*, took Schelling's side against Fichte, and the two friends edited a *Critical Journal of Philosophy* together (1802–1803). For his habilitation Hegel defended a Latin dissertation on the orbits of the planets and twelve theses, the first of which reads *Contradictio est regula veri, non contradictio falsi*: contradiction is the rule of the true, non-contradiction of the false. The dissertation later gave rise to a legend. Giuseppe Piazzi had discovered Ceres, between Mars and Jupiter, on 1 January 1801, and it came to be said that Hegel had proved a priori that no planet could exist there. In fact Hegel criticized the numerical rule that had sent astronomers looking for a planet in that gap and proposed a series adapted from Plato's *Timaeus*, observing only that if his series expressed the order of nature better, there was a large space between the fourth and fifth places and no planet was missing there.

Hegel was finishing the *Phenomenology of Spirit* when Napoleon's army occupied Jena in October 1806. The day before the battle he wrote to his friend Friedrich Immanuel Niethammer:

> Den Kaiser – diese Weltseele – sah ich durch die Stadt zum Rekognoszieren hinausreiten
>
> I saw the Emperor, this world-soul, riding out through the city to reconnoiter
>
> (Letter to Niethammer, 13 October 1806)

The book appeared in 1807. Schelling, who had left Jena in 1803, read its Preface as an attack on himself, and their friendship ended. With no university post available after the war, Hegel edited a newspaper in Bamberg (1807–1808) and then served as rector of a Gymnasium in Nuremberg (1808–1816), where he married Marie von Tucher in 1811 and published the *Science of Logic* (1812–1816). After two years as professor at Heidelberg, where the *Encyclopaedia* appeared (1817), he was called in 1818 to Berlin, to the chair that had been Fichte's. There he published the *Philosophy of Right* (1820) and lectured on the philosophy of history, art, and religion and on the history of philosophy, to audiences that included civil servants as well as students. By the late 1820s he was the most influential philosopher in Germany, at the head of a school. He died in Berlin on 14 November 1831, officially of cholera, and was buried, as he had wished, next to Fichte. His work is customarily divided into three periods.

- **Early (1788–1800).** Tübingen, Bern, and Frankfurt: unpublished writings on religion and politics, on the "positivity" of Christianity, on love and life, and on fate.
- **Jena (1801–1807).** Collaboration with Schelling, drafts of a system, and the break with Schelling in the *Phenomenology*.
- **Mature (1807–1831).** Nuremberg, Heidelberg, and Berlin: the *Science of Logic*, the *Encyclopaedia*, the *Philosophy of Right*, and the Berlin lecture courses.

A week before his death Hegel signed the preface to a revised edition of the Doctrine of Being. Recalling that [[Plato]] was said to have reworked the *Republic* seven times, he wished that a modern work, with a deeper principle, a harder subject, and richer material, could have had the leisure to be reworked seventy-seven times. Much of what is read as Hegel was never published by him at all. After his death a society of his friends and students published his works (1832–1845), including the lectures on history, art, religion, and the history of philosophy, which the editors assembled from Hegel's own notes and from students' transcripts, and the *Zusätze* to the *Encyclopaedia* and the *Philosophy of Right*. The critical edition (GW) publishes the transcripts separately, and scholars now distinguish between what Hegel wrote and what his students heard.

## Starting point: Kant's limits

Hegel's first philosophical book opens its diagnosis of philosophy after Kant with a sentence that could stand over his whole work:

> Entzweiung ist der Quell des Bedürfnisses der Philosophie
>
> Division is the source of the need for philosophy
>
> (Diff)

The divisions Hegel had in mind are the ones that structure the critical philosophy: intuition and concept, appearances and things in themselves, nature and freedom. [[Immanuel Kant|Kant]] drew them in order to secure knowledge within its bounds. Hegel's objection, developed over the next three decades, is that the boundary cannot be drawn the way Kant drew it. He makes the point in three ways.

First, the project of examining our faculty of knowledge before using it is circular, since the examination is itself a use of that faculty.

> Erkennen wollen aber, ehe man erkennt, ist ebenso ungereimt als der weise Vorsatz jenes Scholastikus, schwimmen zu lernen, ehe er sich ins Wasser wage.
>
> But to want to know before one knows is as absurd as the wise resolution of that scholastic to learn to swim before he ventured into the water.
>
> (Enz §10 Anm.)

The Introduction to the *Phenomenology* turns the point against the caution that motivates critique. If knowledge is an instrument for grasping the absolute, or a medium through which its light reaches us, then we never meet the absolute as it is, and any attempt to subtract the instrument's distortion presupposes the very knowledge it doubts (PhG ¶73). The fear of error, Hegel concludes, deserves suspicion in its turn:

> … warum nicht umgekehrt ein Misstrauen in dieses Misstrauen gesetzt und besorgt werden soll, dass diese Furcht zu irren schon der Irrtum selbst ist.
>
> … why one should not, conversely, distrust this distrust and worry that this fear of error is already the error itself.
>
> (PhG ¶74)

Second, to know a limit as a limit is already to be beyond it. To determine our knowledge as finite and restricted is to compare it with something unrestricted, and such knowledge of a limit is possible, Hegel argues, only because the unlimited is already present in consciousness (Enz §60 Anm.). Kant's thing in itself is what remains when every determination has been abstracted away, the *caput mortuum* of abstraction; far from lying beyond thought, it is itself a product of thought (Enz §44 Anm.).

Third, Hegel praised the antinomies as one of Kant's deepest discoveries but rejected what Kant made of them. Kant found contradiction in only four cosmological ideas and blamed it on reason. Hegel held that it is found in all objects of every kind, in all representations, concepts, and ideas, and that Kant had shown "too much tenderness for the world" in removing contradiction from things and placing it in reason alone (Enz §48 Anm.).

## Substance as subject

If the limits of reason are reason's own work, then the absolute cannot be something reason merely confronts. Hegel's account of what it is begins from [[Baruch Spinoza|Spinoza]]. In his lectures he said that whoever begins to philosophize must first be a Spinozist, and that the alternative is "either Spinozism or no philosophy" (VGP). Spinoza's substance is the one reality of which all finite things are modes. What it lacks, for Hegel, is movement: it is the whole only as given, not as the result of its own activity. Schelling's absolute, the point of indifference in which subject and object are identical, repeats the defect, and the Preface to the *Phenomenology* describes such an absolute in its most quoted insult:

> sein Absolutes für die Nacht auszugeben, worin, wie man zu sagen pflegt, alle Kühe schwarz sind, ist die Naivität der Leere an Erkenntnis.
>
> to pass off one's absolute as the night in which, as the saying goes, all cows are black, is the naivety of emptiness in knowledge.
>
> (PhG ¶16)

Against it Hegel states his own principle.

> Es kommt nach meiner Einsicht, welche sich nur durch die Darstellung des Systems selbst rechtfertigen muss, alles darauf an, das Wahre nicht als Substanz, sondern ebenso sehr als Subjekt aufzufassen und auszudrücken.
>
> In my view, which can be justified only by the presentation of the system itself, everything depends on grasping and expressing the true not as substance but equally as subject.
>
> (PhG ¶17)

A subject, in Hegel's sense, is not a mind set over against a world but anything that becomes itself through its own activity: it posits differences within itself and returns to itself out of them. To say that the true is subject is to say that the absolute is not a fixed foundation but a process that comes to know itself, and this is why it is "essentially a result" (PhG ¶20). The Preface's opening image shows what this means for truth. The bud disappears when the blossom breaks forth, and one could say that the blossom refutes the bud; the fruit in turn declares the blossom a false existence of the plant. Yet each stage is as necessary as the others, and this shared necessity makes up the life of the whole (PhG ¶2). Since a result contains the negations through which it was reached, philosophy cannot avoid the negative:

> Aber nicht das Leben, das sich vor dem Tode scheut und von der Verwüstung rein bewahrt, sondern das ihn erträgt und in ihm sich erhält, ist das Leben des Geistes. Er gewinnt seine Wahrheit nur, indem er in der absoluten Zerrissenheit sich selbst findet.
>
> The life of spirit is not the life that shrinks from death and keeps itself untouched by devastation, but the life that endures death and maintains itself in it. Spirit wins its truth only by finding itself in absolute disruption.
>
> (PhG ¶32)

## The Phenomenology: the path of despair

The *Phenomenology* was conceived as the first part of a "system of science" and as its introduction. Ordinary consciousness, Hegel says, has the right to demand that science provide it with a ladder to the standpoint of philosophy (PhG ¶26). Philosophy may not begin "like a shot from a pistol" with absolute knowledge (PhG ¶27), asserting a standpoint that ordinary consciousness lacks. The book climbs the ladder by examining a series of *shapes of consciousness*, each of which takes itself to be knowledge.

The method is internal criticism.

> Das Bewusstsein gibt seinen Maßstab an ihm selbst, und die Untersuchung wird dadurch eine Vergleichung seiner mit sich selbst sein
>
> Consciousness supplies its own standard, and the investigation will therefore be a comparison of consciousness with itself
>
> (PhG ¶84)

Each shape has a conception of what its object is and a conception of what it is to know that object. The philosopher only watches whether the two agree. When they do not, consciousness must change its knowledge, and in doing so it finds that the object, too, was not what it had taken it to be. Hegel gives the process a name.

> Diese dialektische Bewegung, welche das Bewusstsein an ihm selbst, sowohl an seinem Wissen als an seinem Gegenstande ausübt, insofern ihm der neue wahre Gegenstand daraus entspringt, ist eigentlich dasjenige, was Erfahrung genannt wird.
>
> This dialectical movement, which consciousness performs on itself, on its knowledge as well as on its object, insofar as the new true object arises for it out of this movement, is precisely what is called experience.
>
> (PhG ¶86)

Since every shape loses its truth along the way, the road "can be regarded as the path of doubt, or more properly as the path of despair" (PhG ¶78), a "self-consummating skepticism" that, unlike ancient skepticism, ends at each step not in an empty nothing but in a new shape.

| Chapter | Shapes |
| --- | --- |
| I–III. Consciousness | Sense-certainty; perception; force and the understanding |
| IV. Self-consciousness | Lordship and bondage; stoicism, skepticism, the unhappy consciousness |
| V. Reason | Observing reason; the actualization of rational self-consciousness; individuality real in and for itself |
| VI. Spirit | Ethical life; culture; morality |
| VII. Religion | Natural religion; the art-religion; the revealed religion |
| VIII. Absolute knowing | Spirit that knows itself as spirit |

The first shape shows the method at work. *Sense-certainty* takes itself to be the richest knowledge there is, since it holds its object before it in full, with no concept to abstract from it. Asked what it knows, it can say only "this," "here," "now." Hegel proposes a test: write down the truth "Now is night."

> Sehen wir jetzt, diesen Mittag, die aufgeschriebene Wahrheit wieder an, so werden wir sagen müssen, dass sie schal geworden ist.
>
> If we look at the written truth again now, this noon, we will have to say that it has gone stale.
>
> (PhG ¶95)

What survives the test is a now that is indifferently night or day, a universal. Sense-certainty means the singular, but it says the universal, and since language expresses only the universal, "language is the more truthful" (PhG ¶97). What claimed to be the richest knowledge turns out to be "the most abstract and poorest truth" (PhG ¶91), and consciousness passes on to *perception*, which takes its object as a universal thing with many properties.

## Recognition: lordship and bondage

With self-consciousness the object of knowledge becomes the knower itself, and the problem changes. A self-consciousness at first confirms itself by negating what is other than it: "self-consciousness is desire in general" (PhG ¶167). But desire depends on the objects it consumes and must find them again and again. Only another self-consciousness, which can negate itself without ceasing to be, offers a confirmation that does not vanish in the act.

> Das Selbstbewusstsein ist an und für sich, indem und dadurch, dass es für ein Anderes an und für sich ist; d. h. es ist nur als ein Anerkanntes.
>
> Self-consciousness exists in and for itself in that, and by the fact that, it exists in and for itself for another; that is, it exists only as recognized.
>
> (PhG ¶178)

With this, Hegel says, the concept of spirit is already present: "I that is We, and We that is I" (PhG ¶177). What follows shows how far the first attempt at recognition falls short of it. Each self-consciousness must prove to the other, and to itself, that it is not bound to its life, and so each stakes its life in a struggle; only by risking life is freedom proved (PhG ¶187). Death would end the struggle without recognition (PhG ¶188), so it ends instead in an unequal relation. One consciousness, preferring life to freedom, submits. The other becomes its lord (*Herr*), the first its bondsman (*Knecht*).

The relation then reverses. The lord is recognized, but by someone he does not recognize as an equal, so the recognition he receives is one-sided and unequal (PhG ¶191). He enjoys things without working on them, and so has no lasting relation to the world. The bondsman has felt the fear of death, "the absolute lord," which has shaken everything fixed in him (PhG ¶194), and he works.

> Die Arbeit hingegen ist gehemmte Begierde, aufgehaltenes Verschwinden, oder sie bildet.
>
> Work, by contrast, is desire held in check, vanishing staved off: it forms.
>
> (PhG ¶195)

In the things he shapes, the bondsman comes to see his own independence as something objective. "The truth of the independent consciousness is accordingly the servile consciousness" (PhG ¶193). The freedom he gains is still only inner, however. Hegel follows it through [[Stoicism|stoicism]], which declares the mind free whether on the throne or in chains (PhG ¶199); [[Pyrrhonism|skepticism]], which turns that freedom against every determinate thought; and the *unhappy consciousness*, divided between its own changeable self and an unchangeable beyond, the shape Hegel associates with medieval Christianity. In the twentieth century these few paragraphs became the most famous in Hegel, largely through Alexandre Kojève's lectures in Paris (see below), and Kojève's rendering of the pair as *maître* and *esclave* helped make "master and slave" their common name.

## From spirit to absolute knowing

From chapter VI onward the shapes of consciousness are also shapes of a world. *Spirit* begins with Greek ethical life, in which individuals live their community's customs as their own, and Hegel reads its collapse in Sophocles' *Antigone*. The divine law of the family, which Antigone obeys in burying her brother, and the human law of the city, which Creon enforces, are both justified, and their conflict destroys both. Modern spirit is divided against itself. In the culture (*Bildung*) of the old regime individuals win their worth by alienating their natural selves, and the Enlightenment, which dissolves faith in the name of what is useful, ends in the French Revolution's demand for absolute freedom. A freedom that will tolerate no institution can only destroy, and its work is the Terror:

> Es ist also der kälteste, platteste Tod, ohne mehr Bedeutung als das Durchhauen eines Kohlhaupts oder ein Schluck Wassers.
>
> It is thus the coldest, flattest death, with no more significance than cutting off a head of cabbage or swallowing a mouthful of water.
>
> (PhG ¶590)

The moral worldview of Kant and Fichte follows, and then conscience and the romantic "beautiful soul," which preserves its purity by refusing to act. The chapter ends when a consciousness that has acted, and so incurred guilt, confesses, and the one that judged it forgives: "the wounds of spirit heal and leave no scars behind" (PhG ¶669). Religion presents the same reconciliation as a story about God, and absolute knowing grasps it in the form of the concept. Absolute knowing is not knowledge of everything. It is the standpoint at which consciousness no longer takes its object to be something alien to it, because it has come to understand every such opposition as its own work. The book closes with lines adapted from Schiller's poem "Friendship":

> aus dem Kelche dieses Geisterreiches / schäumt ihm seine Unendlichkeit.
>
> from the chalice of this realm of spirits / its infinity foams forth for it.
>
> (PhG ¶808)

At this standpoint the *Science of Logic* begins.

## Dialectic and contradiction

The formula "thesis, antithesis, synthesis," still attached to Hegel's name, is not his. Fichte used the three terms in his *Foundations of the Science of Knowledge* (1794), and Heinrich Moritz Chalybäus popularized them as a summary of Hegel's method in 1837. Hegel himself, in the Preface to the *Phenomenology*, granted the significance of the triadic form that Kant had "rediscovered by instinct" but mocked its reduction to a "lifeless schema" (PhG ¶50). Gustav Mueller called the triad "the Hegel legend" (1958).

Hegel's own account distinguishes three sides of every logical content.

> Das Logische hat der Form nach drei Seiten: α) die abstrakte oder verständige, β) die dialektische oder negativ-vernünftige, γ) die spekulative oder positiv-vernünftige.
>
> In its form, the logical has three sides: (α) the abstract side, or that of the understanding; (β) the dialectical side, or that of negative reason; (γ) the speculative side, or that of positive reason.
>
> (Enz §79)

The understanding fixes determinations and holds them apart (§80). The dialectical moment is their own self-sublation: thought through, each finite determination passes over into its opposite (§81). The speculative moment grasps the unity of the determinations in their opposition, the positive content of their dissolution (§82). These are sides of every concept, not three stages that follow one another, and their result is what Hegel calls *determinate negation*. When a concept or a shape of consciousness fails, the result is not an empty nothing, as for the skeptic, but the negation of that particular content, and so a new content with a determinate place (PhG ¶79).

Contradiction is central to this process, and Hegel meant it to be. Against the law of identity he set the proposition that all things are in themselves contradictory, and he added:

> der Widerspruch aber ist die Wurzel aller Bewegung und Lebendigkeit; nur insofern etwas in sich selbst einen Widerspruch hat, bewegt es sich, hat Trieb und Tätigkeit.
>
> contradiction is the root of all movement and vitality; only insofar as something has a contradiction within itself does it move, have drive and activity.
>
> (WdL, Doctrine of Essence, ch. 2, Remark 3 on contradiction)

His example is motion itself.

> Etwas bewegt sich nur, nicht indem es in diesem Jetzt hier ist und in einem andern Jetzt dort, sondern indem es in einem und demselben Jetzt hier und nicht hier, indem es in diesem Hier zugleich ist und nicht ist.
>
> Something moves not by being here at one now and there at another, but only by being here and not here at one and the same now, by being and not being in this here at the same time.
>
> (WdL, ibid.)

We must grant the ancient dialecticians, [[Eleatic School#Zeno|Zeno]] above all, the contradictions they find in motion, Hegel says; but it does not follow that motion does not exist, only that motion is existing contradiction itself (WdL, ibid.). Two objections from logic have dogged the doctrine. The first concerns motion. [[Bertrand Russell|Russell]], writing with Weierstrass's analysis behind him, held that motion consists simply in being at different places at different times (*The Principles of Mathematics*, 1903). A motion is a function $x$ from times to positions, and its velocity at an instant,
$$
v(t) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x(t+h) - x(t)}{h},
$$
is fixed by the values of $x$ at times near $t$, without the body's being in two states at once. The second objection is more general. [[Karl Popper|Popper]] pointed out in "What Is Dialectic?" (1940) that in classical logic a contradiction entails everything: from $A$ one may infer $A \lor B$, and from $A \lor B$ together with $\neg A$ one may infer $B$, whatever $B$ is. A theory that accepts contradictions therefore accepts every statement and says nothing.

Hegel's defenders have answered in two ways. Some deny that his contradictions are conjunctions of the form $A \land \neg A$ at all: a contradiction in Hegel's sense is an opposition within a concept or a thing that drives it beyond itself, not a pair of assertions to be believed together. Others accept the letter of the doctrine and give up classical logic instead. In the paraconsistent logics developed since the late 1940s by Stanisław Jaśkowski, Newton da Costa, and others, a contradiction does not entail everything, and Graham Priest's *In Contradiction* (1987), which defends the view that some contradictions are true, claims Hegel as a predecessor.

## The Logic: being, nothing, becoming

The *Science of Logic* is not a logic in the modern sense, a theory of valid inference, although it contains one. It is the system of the pure determinations of thought, the concepts without which nothing can be thought at all, developed in the order in which each arises from the one before. Since absolute knowing has left behind the opposition between thought and its object, these determinations are, for Hegel, at the same time the determinations of what is. The first two of its three books, the "objective logic," take the place of the old metaphysics, ontology above all. Hegel described its content in a sentence that has been quoted ever since, with admiration and with alarm:

> Man kann sich deswegen ausdrücken, dass dieser Inhalt die Darstellung Gottes ist, wie er in seinem ewigen Wesen vor der Erschaffung der Natur und eines endlichen Geistes ist.
>
> One may therefore say that this content is the presentation of God as he is in his eternal essence before the creation of nature and of a finite spirit.
>
> (WdL, Introduction)

A science without presuppositions must begin with what presupposes nothing, the most immediate and indeterminate thought there is (WdL, "With What Must the Beginning of Science Be Made?"). The Logic therefore opens with a sentence that has no verb:

> Sein, reines Sein, – ohne alle weitere Bestimmung.
>
> Being, pure being, without any further determination.
>
> (WdL, Doctrine of Being, ch. 1)

Pure being has no content that would distinguish it from anything else; to think it is to think nothing in particular.

> Das Sein, das unbestimmte Unmittelbare, ist in der Tat Nichts, und nicht mehr noch weniger als Nichts.
>
> Being, the indeterminate immediate, is in fact nothing, and neither more nor less than nothing.
>
> (WdL, ibid.)

Nothing, thought in turn, is the same empty indeterminacy, and so it is being. Yet the two are not simply identical, since each is thought as the opposite of the other. What thought actually finds is the movement of each into the other:

> Die Wahrheit ist also nicht das Sein noch das Nichts, sondern dass das Sein in Nichts und das Nichts in Sein – nicht übergeht, sondern übergegangen ist.
>
> The truth is therefore neither being nor nothing, but that being has passed over into nothing and nothing into being; not that it passes over, but that it has passed over.
>
> (WdL, ibid.)

This movement is *becoming*, the first concrete thought. The beginning restages the opening of Greek philosophy: [[Eleatic School#Parmenides|Parmenides]] thought pure being and denied nothing, and [[Heraclitus]] taught that all is becoming. Of Heraclitus Hegel said, "here we see land; there is no proposition of Heraclitus that I have not taken up into my Logic" (VGP). The term Hegel uses for what happens to each earlier stage became the most characteristic word of his philosophy.

> Aufheben hat in der Sprache den gedoppelten Sinn, dass es so viel als aufbewahren, erhalten bedeutet und zugleich so viel als aufhören lassen, ein Ende machen.
>
> In ordinary language, aufheben has the double sense of preserving, keeping, and at the same time of letting cease, putting an end to.
>
> (WdL, Doctrine of Being, ch. 1, Remark on the expression "sublate")

A sublated determination is negated as an independent truth and preserved as a moment of a richer one. The three books of the Logic differ in how one determination leads to the next.

| Book | Divisions | Movement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Being | Quality; quantity; measure | Passing over (*Übergehen*) into an other |
| Essence | Essence as reflection; appearance; actuality | Shining (*Scheinen*) in an other |
| Concept | Subjectivity; objectivity; the Idea | Development (*Entwicklung*) |

In the doctrine of being, each determination simply becomes another, as being becomes nothing. In the doctrine of essence, determinations come in pairs, such as identity and difference, ground and grounded, whole and parts, each of which is what it is only by reflecting the other. In the doctrine of the concept the movement is development: what comes later only unfolds what was already there (Enz §161). The book ends with the *absolute Idea*, the concept that has for its content the method of the Logic itself.

Already in its second chapter the Logic meets the problem of the infinite. A something is limited by an other, which is limited in turn, and so on without end. Hegel calls this endless progress the *bad* infinite (*schlechte Unendlichkeit*, Enz §94). It is the mere negation of the finite, an infinite that is always beyond and never reached, and so itself something finite, bounded by the finite it excludes. The *true* infinite does not stand beyond the finite but contains it as a moment, as the finite's own going beyond itself and returning to itself. The image of the endless progress is a straight line running out indefinitely; for the true infinite Hegel has another.

> als wahrhafte Unendlichkeit, in sich zurückgebogen, wird deren Bild der Kreis, die sich erreicht habende Linie, die geschlossen und ganz gegenwärtig ist, ohne Anfangspunkt und Ende.
>
> as true infinity, bent back into itself, its image becomes the circle, the line that has reached itself, closed and wholly present, without beginning or end.
>
> (WdL, Doctrine of Being, ch. 2)

### Remark: two infinities in one fraction

Hegel returns to the distinction in the doctrine of quantity, in a long Remark on the mathematical infinite, and gives two examples of a quantity written in two ways (WdL, Doctrine of Being, section 2, ch. 2, Remark 1). The fraction $2/7$ can be written as the decimal $0.285714\ldots$, and $1/(1-a)$ as the series $1 + a + a^2 + a^3 + \cdots$. The series, Hegel argues, is a bad infinite: however far it is continued, something is still missing, and its sum remains something that ought to be reached and never is. The fraction states the same quantity completely, as a relation between finite terms, and in this Hegel sees the structure of the true infinite.

The first half of the claim can be made exact.

**Proposition.** Let $a$ be a real number with $0 < |a| < 1$, and let $s_n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} a^k$. Then for every $n \ge 0$,
$$
\frac{1}{1-a} - s_n = \frac{a^{n+1}}{1-a} \neq 0,
$$
and $s_n \to 1/(1-a)$ as $n \to \infty$.

**Proof.** Since $(1-a)\, s_n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} a^k - \sum_{k=1}^{n+1} a^k = 1 - a^{n+1}$, we have $s_n = (1 - a^{n+1})/(1-a)$, which gives the identity. Its right-hand side is nonzero because $a \neq 0$, and it tends to $0$ because $|a|^{n+1} \to 0$. $\blacksquare$

No partial sum reaches the value, exactly as Hegel says. Yet the value is fully determinate, and modern analysis does not need the infinitely many additions to be completed. Since Cauchy's *Cours d'analyse* (1821), nine years after the Doctrine of Being first appeared, the sum of a series has been defined as the limit $s$ of its partial sums, which is fixed by one condition on all of them at once: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is an $N$ such that $|s_n - s| < \varepsilon$ for all $n \ge N$. The limit concept thus states the "beyond" of the series in finite form, which is what Hegel claimed for the fraction.

Hegel's two examples are in fact one. Since $7 \cdot 285714 = 1999998 = 2\,(10^6 - 1)$,
$$
\frac{2}{7} = \frac{285714}{10^6 - 1} = 285714 \left( 10^{-6} + 10^{-12} + 10^{-18} + \cdots \right),
$$
which is the second example with $a = 10^{-6}$, multiplied by a block of six digits. The repetition has a mechanism that suits Hegel's image of the true infinite as a circle. In the long division of $2$ by $7$, each remainder determines the next by $r_{k+1} \equiv 10\, r_k \pmod 7$, and each digit is $d_{k+1} = \lfloor 10\, r_k / 7 \rfloor$. Starting from $r_0 = 2$, the remainders run
$$
2 \to 6 \to 4 \to 5 \to 1 \to 3 \to 2,
$$
producing the digits $2, 8, 5, 7, 1, 4$, and then the cycle closes. In general, if $0 < p < q$ and $\gcd(p, q) = \gcd(10, q) = 1$, multiplication by $10$ permutes the finite set $\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}$, so the remainders of $p/q$ must return to $p$; the decimal expansion of $p/q$ is therefore purely periodic, and its period is the order of $10$ modulo $q$. This is the finite-state argument of the remark in the [[Friedrich Nietzsche|Nietzsche]] post: a bijection of a finite set gives every state an infinite past, so every state recurs. %% link: decimal expansions in Number Theory %%

Whether a series reaches its value at all depends on how distance is measured. For $a = 2$ the partial sums are $s_n = 2^{n+1} - 1$, which diverge in $\mathbb{R}$, although $1/(1-a) = -1$. In the field $\mathbb{Q}_2$ of $2$-adic numbers, where a nonzero rational $x = 2^{v} u / w$ with $v \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $u, w$ odd has absolute value $|x|_2 = 2^{-v}$, the same computation gives
$$
\bigl| s_n - (-1) \bigr|_2 = \bigl| 2^{n+1} \bigr|_2 = 2^{-(n+1)} \to 0,
$$
so that $1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + \cdots = -1$ holds there. By Ostrowski's theorem (1916), every nontrivial absolute value on $\mathbb{Q}$ is equivalent either to the usual one or to a $p$-adic one $|\cdot|_p$, and in each corresponding completion the series $\sum a^k$ converges, with sum $1/(1-a)$, exactly when $|a| < 1$ there. The fraction is the same rational number in all of these fields; what its series amounts to depends on the field. This can be read as a modest point in Hegel's favor: the relation comes first, and the endless expansion expresses it only relative to a choice of context. Hegel's much longer Remarks on the differential calculus, which discuss Newton, Leibniz, and Lagrange, were written before Cauchy and Weierstrass gave the calculus its modern foundations, and they are now read more for their philosophy than for their mathematics. %% link: geometric series in Analysis; p-adic numbers in Number Theory %%

## Nature and spirit

The *Encyclopaedia* presents the whole system in outline, in three parts: the logic, the philosophy of nature, and the philosophy of spirit. If the Logic presents the Idea in the element of pure thought, nature is "the Idea in the form of otherness" (Enz §247): the same rational determinations, but outside one another, in space and time, and burdened with contingency. Hegel speaks of the "impotence of nature" (*Ohnmacht der Natur*), which cannot hold fast to the concept in its particular forms (Enz §250). Philosophy does not deduce those particulars. When the philosopher Wilhelm Traugott Krug challenged the idealists to deduce his writing pen, Hegel replied in a footnote that Krug might be given hope of this achievement once science had advanced so far that it had nothing more important to comprehend (Enz §250 Anm.). The philosophy of nature is nonetheless the part of the system that has fared worst. Its mechanics defended Kepler against Newton, and its optics sided with Goethe's theory of colors against Newton's.

Spirit is the truth of nature: the Idea that has returned to itself out of its otherness. Its philosophy has three parts.

| Spirit | Divisions | Concern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subjective | Anthropology; phenomenology; psychology | The individual mind: soul, consciousness, intelligence and will |
| Objective | Abstract right; morality; ethical life | Freedom made actual in institutions |
| Absolute | Art; religion; philosophy | Spirit knowing itself |

The *Phenomenology* reappears here as a section of the philosophy of subjective spirit, now covering only consciousness, self-consciousness, and reason. The rest of this post follows objective and absolute spirit, which Hegel worked out at length in his Berlin books and lectures.

## Freedom and the state

> Der Boden des Rechts ist überhaupt das Geistige und seine nähere Stelle und Ausgangspunkt der Wille, welcher frei ist, so dass die Freiheit seine Substanz und Bestimmung ausmacht und das Rechtssystem das Reich der verwirklichten Freiheit, die Welt des Geistes aus ihm selbst hervorgebracht, als eine zweite Natur, ist.
>
> The ground of right is in general the spiritual, and its more precise place and point of departure is the will, which is free, so that freedom constitutes its substance and its determination, and the system of right is the realm of actualized freedom, the world of spirit brought forth out of itself as a second nature.
>
> (GPR §4)

The *Philosophy of Right* is Hegel's account of objective spirit, the institutions in which freedom becomes actual. It has three parts. *Abstract right* treats persons as bearers of rights: property, contract, wrong, and punishment. *Morality* treats the subject's inner will, its intentions and its conscience, and it is here that Hegel criticizes [[Immanuel Kant|Kant]]'s moral philosophy as an empty formalism. The test of universalizability, he argues, can show only that a maxim is consistent with itself, and consistency yields no content: that there should be no property at all contains no more contradiction than that this or that people or family should not exist (GPR §135 Anm.). Content comes only in *ethical life* (*Sittlichkeit*), the shared institutions in which duties are concrete: the family, civil society, and the state.

*Civil society* is Hegel's name for the modern sphere of the market, in which individuals pursue their own ends and, through the division of labor, satisfy one another's needs. Hegel had read Adam Smith, Jean-Baptiste Say, and David Ricardo (GPR §189 Anm.), and he saw clearly what the market could not do:

> dass bei dem Übermaße des Reichtums die bürgerliche Gesellschaft nicht reich genug ist, … dem Übermaße der Armut und der Erzeugung des Pöbels zu steuern.
>
> that despite an excess of wealth, civil society is not rich enough … to check the excess of poverty and the creation of a rabble.
>
> (GPR §245)

The *state* is "the actuality of the ethical Idea" (GPR §257), the community in which individuals know the universal as their own end. A sentence from the Zusatz to §258, assembled from students' notes and long rendered as "the state is the march of God on earth," says rather that it is the way of God in the world that there should be a state. Hegel credited [[Jean-Jacques Rousseau|Rousseau]] with making the will the principle of the state but objected that Rousseau's general will was only a common will compounded out of individual wills, a contract, and that its application in the French Revolution ended in terror (GPR §258 Anm.).

The Preface was written in 1820, a year after the Carlsbad Decrees had tightened censorship and the supervision of universities in the German states, and it contains the book's most disputed sentence:

> Was vernünftig ist, das ist wirklich; und was wirklich ist, das ist vernünftig.
>
> What is rational is actual; and what is actual is rational.
>
> (GPR Preface)

Hegel later protested that he had not meant whatever happens to exist: a contingent existence, he insisted, does not deserve the emphatic name of the actual (Enz §6 Anm.). Read his way, the sentence says that reason has the power to realize itself and that what is truly actual is rational; read the other way, it sanctifies the status quo. The Preface also limits what philosophy can do. Philosophy is "its own time comprehended in thoughts," and it cannot instruct the world how it ought to be, because it always comes too late:

> Die Eule der Minerva beginnt erst mit der einbrechenden Dämmerung ihren Flug.
>
> The owl of Minerva begins its flight only with the falling of dusk.
>
> (GPR Preface)

Rudolf Haym (*Hegel und seine Zeit*, 1857) branded Hegel the philosopher of the Prussian Restoration, and [[Karl Popper|Popper]] (*The Open Society and Its Enemies*, 1945) made him a forerunner of modern totalitarianism. Shlomo Avineri (*Hegel's Theory of the Modern State*, 1972) and others have pointed out that the state Hegel describes had institutions that Prussia as a whole lacked in 1820, among them a written constitution, a national assembly of estates, and public trials with juries, and that its monarch's role, according to a student's note, was only to "dot the i" (GPR §280 Zusatz).

## History

> Die Weltgeschichte ist der Fortschritt im Bewusstsein der Freiheit – ein Fortschritt, den wir in seiner Notwendigkeit zu erkennen haben.
>
> World history is the progress of the consciousness of freedom, a progress that we have to know in its necessity.
>
> (VPG, Introduction)

Hegel lectured on the philosophy of history five times in Berlin, and the Introduction to these lectures is the most accessible thing he wrote. His scheme is simple. The Orient knew only that one is free, the despot; the Greek and Roman world knew that some are free; and the Christian, Germanic world knows that all human beings as such are free. History is the process by which this knowledge becomes actual in institutions. It does not happen through good intentions. Nothing great in the world, Hegel says, has been accomplished without passion, and the passions of individuals serve ends they do not know. Reason lets the passions work for it, and Hegel calls this the *cunning of reason* (VPG, Introduction). World-historical individuals, such as Alexander, Caesar, and Napoleon, are those whose private aims coincide with what their age requires; once they have accomplished it, they fall away like empty husks.

The scheme has a price that Hegel did not hide.

> Die Weltgeschichte ist nicht der Boden des Glücks. Die Perioden des Glücks sind leere Blätter in ihr.
>
> World history is not the soil of happiness. Periods of happiness are blank pages in it.
>
> (VPG, Introduction)

He called the result a theodicy, a justification of God in history, taking up [[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz|Leibniz]]'s word for a vindication of God in the face of evil. The lectures also have a price Hegel did not see. Working from the travel literature available to him, he declared that Africa is "no historical part of the world" and treated China and India as the stationary beginning of history, judgments now widely criticized as Eurocentric and racist. Susan Buck-Morss has argued that the dialectic of lordship and bondage, written while the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804) was in the news, was shaped by it; the thesis remains contested.

## Art, religion, philosophy

Absolute spirit is spirit that knows itself, and it does so in three forms with one content: the intuition of art, the representation (*Vorstellung*) of religion, and the concept of philosophy.

Art is "the sensuous shining of the Idea" (VÄ): truth presented in a form that can be seen or heard. Hegel's lectures on aesthetics divide its history into three forms. In *symbolic* art, above all that of Egypt, meaning still struggles with a material that cannot fully express it. In *classical* art, the sculpture of the Greek gods, meaning and form are in perfect balance. In *romantic* art, the painting, music, and poetry of the Christian era, spirit has become too inward for any sensuous form to contain. Hence the lectures' famous verdict:

> In allen diesen Beziehungen ist und bleibt die Kunst nach der Seite ihrer höchsten Bestimmung für uns ein Vergangenes.
>
> In all these respects art, considered in its highest vocation, is and remains for us a thing of the past.
>
> (VÄ, Introduction)

Hegel did not mean that art would cease to be made, but that it can no longer be the highest way in which a culture grasps its truth. Religion grasps the same truth in representations, in stories and images, and Christianity, the "consummate religion," presents the reconciliation of the infinite and the finite as the incarnation, death, and resurrection of God. Hegel took the death seriously. Already in *Faith and Knowledge* he wrote of

> das Gefühl, worauf die Religion der neuen Zeit beruht, – das Gefühl: Gott selbst ist tot
>
> the feeling on which the religion of the modern age rests: the feeling that God himself is dead
>
> (GuW)

borrowing the line from a Lutheran Good Friday hymn, and he asked philosophy to restore a "speculative Good Friday," in which that death is thought as a moment of the divine life rather than its end. Eighty years later [[Friedrich Nietzsche|Nietzsche]]'s madman announced the same death without the resurrection.

Philosophy, finally, grasps in concepts what religion represents. Its history, in Hegel's lectures, is not a gallery of opinions but the development of one philosophy through successive systems, each of which is preserved as a moment in the next. With [[René Descartes|Descartes]], he said, philosophy reaches home ground and can cry "Land!" like a sailor after a long voyage on a stormy sea (VGP). The *Encyclopaedia* ends not with Hegel's own words but with a passage in Greek from [[Aristotle]]'s *Metaphysics* (Λ 7, 1072b18–30), on the divine thinking that thinks itself (Enz §577).

## After Hegel

- Hegel's school split within a few years of his death. David Friedrich Strauss's *Life of Jesus* (1835), which treated the Gospels as myth, forced the question of how Hegel's philosophy stood to Christianity, and Strauss himself, borrowing from the seating of the French parliament, divided the Hegelians into a right, a center, and a left (1837). The Right read Hegel as reconciling philosophy with Christianity and the existing state; the Young Hegelians, among them Bruno Bauer, Arnold Ruge, and Max Stirner, turned his method against both.
- [[Ludwig Feuerbach|Feuerbach]] (*Toward a Critique of Hegel's Philosophy*, 1839; *The Essence of Christianity*, 1841) argued that speculative philosophy, like theology, projects the human essence onto an alien subject, and that the secret of theology is anthropology.
- [[Karl Marx|Marx]] took over the dialectic and rejected its idealism. With Hegel, he wrote, the dialectic stands on its head, and it must be turned right side up to discover the rational kernel within the mystical shell (afterword to the second edition of *Capital*, 1873).
- [[Søren Kierkegaard|Kierkegaard]], who heard Schelling lecture against Hegel in Berlin in 1841–42, as did Friedrich Engels and Mikhail Bakunin, answered the system with the existing individual: a logical system is possible, but a system of existence is not (*Concluding Unscientific Postscript*, 1846).
- [[Arthur Schopenhauer|Schopenhauer]], who as a young lecturer in Berlin in 1820 scheduled his course at the same hour as Hegel's and found almost no students, called him a charlatan for the rest of his life.
- [[Friedrich Nietzsche|Nietzsche]] wrote that we Germans are Hegelians even if there had never been a Hegel, insofar as we instinctively attribute a deeper meaning and a richer value to becoming and development than to what "is" (FW §357).
- In Britain, idealists from T. H. Green and F. H. Bradley to J. M. E. McTaggart dominated philosophy at the end of the nineteenth century. Analytic philosophy began when [[Bertrand Russell|Russell]] and G. E. Moore broke with this idealism around 1898.
- In France, Jean Wahl's *The Unhappy Consciousness in Hegel's Philosophy* (1929) and above all Alexandre Kojève's lectures on the *Phenomenology* in Paris (1933–1939), attended by [[Jacques Lacan|Lacan]], Georges Bataille, Raymond Queneau, [[Maurice Merleau-Ponty|Merleau-Ponty]], and Raymond Aron, made the struggle for recognition and the end of history central to a generation. [[Jean-Paul Sartre|Sartre]]'s account of being-for-others in *Being and Nothingness* (1943) builds on the dialectic of lordship and bondage, and Kojève's reading lies behind Francis Fukuyama's "The End of History?" (1989).
- [[Martin Heidegger|Heidegger]] treated Hegel's concept of time as the culmination of the ordinary conception (SZ §82) and wrote a long commentary on the Introduction to the *Phenomenology*, "Hegel's Concept of Experience" (1942–43).
- Theodor W. Adorno inverted the Preface's motto: "Das Ganze ist das Unwahre," the whole is the untrue (*Minima Moralia*, 1951). [[Emmanuel Levinas|Levinas]]'s *Totality and Infinity* (1961) set the relation to the other person against every totality, and [[Michel Foucault|Foucault]] said in his inaugural lecture at the Collège de France (1970) that our entire age, whether through logic or epistemology, through Marx or Nietzsche, is trying to escape Hegel. [[Gilles Deleuze|Deleuze]]'s *Difference and Repetition* (1968) and [[Jacques Derrida|Derrida]]'s *Glas* (1974) are two of the most ambitious attempts.
- Since the 1980s, Robert Pippin, Terry Pinkard, Robert Brandom, and John McDowell have revived Hegel within analytic philosophy, reading him as continuing Kant's account of concepts and self-consciousness rather than as a speculative metaphysician, a reading that others contest.

## Reading order

The Introduction to the *Lectures on the Philosophy of History* is the gentlest way in, and the Preface and Introduction to the *Phenomenology*, though difficult, state the whole program. In the *Phenomenology* itself, chapters I–IV (¶¶90–230), from sense-certainty to the unhappy consciousness, show the method at work, and chapter VI is the best guide to Hegel's view of history. For the Logic, the shorter *Encyclopaedia Logic*, especially its "Preliminary Conception" (§§19–83) with its survey of the attitudes of thought toward objectivity, comes before the *Science of Logic*. The Preface and Introduction to the *Philosophy of Right* (§§1–33) and its section on civil society (§§182–256) can be read on their own.
