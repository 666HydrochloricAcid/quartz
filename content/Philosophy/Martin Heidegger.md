Martin Heidegger (1889–1976) spent his working life on one question: what do we mean when we say that something *is*? In this series he stands at a crossroads. He took his question from [[Aristotle]] by way of [[Franz Brentano|Brentano]] and his method from [[Edmund Husserl|Husserl]], and he read [[Immanuel Kant|Kant]]'s *Critique* as a groundwork of ontology and [[Friedrich Nietzsche|Nietzsche]] as the last metaphysician. Almost everyone who comes after him in this series defines a position against him: [[Jean-Paul Sartre|Sartre]] by turning his analysis into an existentialism, [[Emmanuel Levinas|Levinas]] by putting ethics before ontology, [[Maurice Merleau-Ponty|Merleau-Ponty]] by giving being-in-the-world a body, and later [[Jacques Derrida|Derrida]] and [[Michel Foucault|Foucault]].

This post follows the question of being through its two main phases. *Being and Time* (1927) approaches being through the one being that understands being, namely ourselves. Its analysis finds that our being is care and that the meaning of care is temporality, and it was meant to end by showing that time is the horizon within which being as such is understood. That last step was never published. After what he called the "turn," Heidegger stopped approaching being from our understanding of it and asked instead how being has shown itself in the history of the West, from the early Greeks to modern technology. The post also cannot avoid 1933. Heidegger's engagement with National Socialism, and the antisemitic passages in his notebooks, are part of how he is read.

## Life

Heidegger was born on 26 September 1889 in Meßkirch, a small Catholic town in Baden, where his father was sexton of the church of St. Martin. Intended for the priesthood, he entered the Jesuit novitiate in 1909 but left within weeks for reasons of health, and he studied theology at Freiburg until 1911. He then turned to philosophy, while also studying mathematics and the natural sciences. Two early encounters shaped the question that occupied him for the rest of his life. In 1907 a priest who had taken an interest in him, Conrad Gröber, gave him Brentano's dissertation *On the Several Senses of Being in Aristotle* (1862), and as a student he read Husserl's *Logical Investigations*.

His doctoral dissertation (1913) criticized psychologistic theories of judgment, and his habilitation thesis (1915) treated the doctrine of categories and meaning attributed to Duns Scotus, much of it based on a treatise later shown to be by Thomas of Erfurt. After war service he became Husserl's assistant in Freiburg (1919–1923), then taught at Marburg (1923–1928). *Being and Time* appeared in 1927, dedicated to Husserl, and in 1928 Heidegger succeeded Husserl in his chair at Freiburg. Hannah Arendt, Hans-Georg Gadamer, Karl Löwith, Hans Jonas, and Herbert Marcuse were among his students in Marburg and Freiburg. His work is customarily divided into three periods.

- **Early (to 1927).** From the logic of judgment and scholastic theories of meaning to the "hermeneutics of facticity" of the Freiburg and Marburg lecture courses, culminating in *Being and Time*.
- **Middle (1927–1936).** Attempts to complete the project of *Being and Time*, through Kant and through the problem of the nothing, and the political episode of 1933–34.
- **Late (1936–1976).** The history of being, worked out in private manuscripts and in lectures on Nietzsche and Hölderlin, and after the war in essays on art, technology, language, and dwelling.

In April 1933 Heidegger was elected rector of the University of Freiburg, and on 1 May he joined the National Socialist party, of which he remained a member until 1945. His rectoral address, "The Self-Assertion of the German University," placed the university's tasks in the service of the new state. He resigned the rectorate after a year. Husserl, who was of Jewish descent, was pushed out of German academic life by the racial laws; Heidegger did not attend his funeral in 1938, and the 1941 edition of *Being and Time* appeared without the dedication, at the publisher's request according to Heidegger. After the war the French occupation authorities barred him from teaching, and he resumed lecturing only in 1951, as professor emeritus. He spent much of his later life in a hut he had built at Todtnauberg in the Black Forest, and he died in Freiburg on 26 May 1976.

The relation between the philosophy and the politics has been debated ever since. Löwith reported that when they met in Rome in 1936, Heidegger agreed that his political engagement was grounded in his concept of historicity. The question became sharper with the publication, from 2014, of the *Black Notebooks*, the philosophical notebooks Heidegger kept from the early 1930s. They contain antisemitic passages that assign "world Judaism" a place in his history of being, and their editor, Peter Trawny, called this a "being-historical antisemitism." Readers still divide between those who think the analyses of *Being and Time* can be separated from these commitments and those who think they cannot.

## The question of being

*Being and Time* opens with a passage from Plato's *Sophist* in which the Stranger confesses that the word "being," which once seemed familiar, has become a source of perplexity (244a). Heidegger draws the consequence for his own time:

> Und so gilt es denn, die Frage nach dem Sinn von Sein erneut zu stellen.
>
> So it is necessary to pose the question of the meaning of being anew.
>
> (SZ 1)

The question has been neglected, Heidegger argues, because three prejudices make it look empty (SZ §1). Being is the most universal concept; it is indefinable; and it is self-evident, since everyone understands "the sky is blue" or "I am glad." None of these makes the question idle. Universality is not clarity, and it has been known since Aristotle that being is not a genus (SZ 3). Indefinability shows only that being cannot be defined the way beings are, by genus and specific difference. And the self-evidence of our everyday understanding of being, combined with our inability to say what it is, is the problem itself.

The book's guiding distinction is stated early.

> Das Sein des Seienden „ist" nicht selbst ein Seiendes.
>
> The being of beings "is" not itself a being.
>
> (SZ 6)

This is the *ontological difference* between being (*Sein*) and beings (*Seiendes*). Tables, numbers, people, and God are beings; being is what is meant by their *being* at all, in their different ways. The first philosophical step, Heidegger says, is not to "tell a story," that is, not to explain beings as beings by tracing them back to some other being (SZ 6). Metaphysics has repeatedly done exactly this when it identified being with a highest being, substance, or the subject. Brentano's dissertation had shown him Aristotle's version of the problem: being "is said in many ways" (*Metaphysics* Γ 2, 1003a33), and the question is what unifies these senses.

Where should the question begin? With the being that asks it.

> Das Dasein ist ein Seiendes, das nicht nur unter anderem Seienden vorkommt. Es ist vielmehr dadurch ontisch ausgezeichnet, dass es diesem Seienden in seinem Sein um dieses Sein selbst geht.
>
> Dasein is a being that does not simply occur among other beings. What marks it out ontically is rather that, in its being, this being is concerned with that very being.
>
> (SZ 12)

*Dasein*, ordinary German for "existence," literally "being-there," is Heidegger's name for the being we ourselves are. He uses it to avoid "man," "subject," and "consciousness," each of which carries a metaphysical interpretation he wants to suspend. Because the understanding of being is itself part of Dasein's way of being (SZ 12), the question of being must begin with an analysis of Dasein, which Heidegger calls *fundamental ontology* (SZ 13). Two pairs of terms keep the levels apart. *Ontic* inquiry concerns beings, *ontological* inquiry their being. An *existentiell* understanding is a particular person's grasp of their own concrete possibilities; an *existential* analysis concerns the structures that any such existence has.

The method is phenomenology, whose maxim Heidegger takes from Husserl: "to the things themselves!" (SZ 27). He defines it from the Greek as letting what shows itself be seen from itself, in the way it shows itself from itself (SZ 34), and concludes that ontology is possible only as phenomenology (SZ 35). Because Dasein always already understands itself in some way, the phenomenology of Dasein is also *hermeneutic*, an interpretation (SZ 37). It was to be complemented by a *destruction* (*Destruktion*) of the history of ontology, a dismantling of traditional concepts back to the experiences from which they arose, which Heidegger insists is not negative in intent (SZ 22).

## Being-in-the-world

> Das „Wesen" des Daseins liegt in seiner Existenz.
>
> The "essence" of Dasein lies in its existence.
>
> (SZ 42)

The characteristics of Dasein are not properties of something present, the way a stone has a weight, but possible ways for it to be. Dasein's being is in each case mine (*Jemeinigkeit*, SZ 42), and Dasein can win itself or lose itself, so it can exist *authentically* (*eigentlich*, from *eigen*, "own") or *inauthentically*. Heidegger insists that inauthenticity is not a lesser degree of being (SZ 43); it is the way Dasein mostly is. Since the structures of Dasein are not properties, they are not categories either. Heidegger calls them *existentials* (*Existenzialien*, SZ 44).

Dasein's basic state is *being-in-the-world* (*In-der-Welt-sein*), a unitary phenomenon and not a subject plus a world (SZ 53). The "in" is not the containment of water in a glass. Heidegger traces it to an old sense of dwelling, being familiar with, being at home in (SZ 54).

The world first shows up not as a collection of objects but as equipment (*Zeug*). Equipment is essentially "something in order to …" (SZ 68): the hammer is for driving nails, the nail for fastening boards, the boards for building a shelter. In using the hammer I do not observe it; the more I simply hammer, the more the hammer is encountered as what it is (SZ 69). Heidegger calls this mode of being *readiness-to-hand* (*Zuhandenheit*) and distinguishes it from *presence-at-hand* (*Vorhandenheit*), the being of things considered as bearers of properties.

| | Readiness-to-hand (*Zuhandenheit*) | Presence-at-hand (*Vorhandenheit*) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Access | Use, guided by circumspection (*Umsicht*) | Looking at, theoretical observation |
| What shows up | Equipment within a network of "in order to" | A thing with properties |
| Example | "Too heavy. Give me the other hammer!" | "The hammer is heavy." |

The example in the last row is Heidegger's own (SZ 157). The theoretical assertion is derived from the practical judgment, not the other way round. Presence-at-hand comes to the fore when equipment breaks down. A tool that is damaged becomes conspicuous, one that is missing becomes obtrusive, one that is in the way becomes obstinate (SZ 73–74), and in each case the referential context that use had taken for granted lights up. That context, a totality of references ending in a "for-the-sake-of-which" (*Worumwillen*), some possibility of Dasein itself, is what Heidegger means by the *worldhood* of the world (SZ 84).

This is why Heidegger thinks [[René Descartes|Descartes]] missed the world: by defining the world as extended substance, he began from presence-at-hand and passed over the phenomenon of world altogether (SZ §§19–21). It is also why he answers [[Immanuel Kant|Kant]] the way he does. Kant had called it a scandal of philosophy that the existence of things outside us must be accepted merely on faith, and he offered a proof (KrV Bxxxix).

> Der „Skandal der Philosophie" besteht nicht darin, dass dieser Beweis bislang noch aussteht, sondern darin, dass solche Beweise immer wieder erwartet und versucht werden.
>
> The "scandal of philosophy" is not that this proof is still lacking, but that people keep expecting and attempting such proofs.
>
> (SZ 205)

A subject that first had to prove the existence of a world would be a subject without a world. Dasein, correctly understood, already is what such proofs try to establish.

Being-in-the-world is also being-with (*Mitsein*): others are there in the world of equipment from the start, as those for whom the shelter is built or who supplied the boards (SZ 118–120). In everyday life, however, who I am is mostly determined by no one in particular. We enjoy ourselves as *one* enjoys oneself, read and judge literature as *one* reads and judges it (SZ 126–127). Heidegger calls this anonymous subject *das Man*, the "one" or the "they."

> Jeder ist der Andere und Keiner er selbst.
>
> Each one is the other, and no one is himself.
>
> (SZ 128)

## Mood, understanding, and the circle

Dasein is disclosed to itself in three equally original ways. The first is *attunement* (*Befindlichkeit*). We are always in some mood, and a mood discloses something no theoretical attitude can: that I am and have to be, without having chosen to be at all. Heidegger calls this *thrownness* (*Geworfenheit*, SZ 135). The second is *understanding*, which is not primarily cognition but a projection (*Entwurf*) of Dasein onto its possibilities. Only because Dasein is its possibilities can it say to itself, in Pindar's words, "become what you are!" (SZ 145), a phrase [[Friedrich Nietzsche|Nietzsche]] had also made his own (FW §270, and the subtitle of *Ecce homo*). The third is *discourse* (*Rede*), the articulation of what is understood.

Understanding develops into interpretation, in which something is taken *as* something, and every interpretation operates within a fore-structure of what it already has in view, how it sees it, and how it conceives it (SZ 150). Interpretation therefore moves in a circle, which looks like a vicious one if scientific proof is the model.

> Das Entscheidende ist nicht, aus dem Zirkel heraus-, sondern in ihn nach der rechten Weise hineinzukommen.
>
> What matters is not to get out of the circle but to get into it in the right way.
>
> (SZ 153)

For the most part, Dasein has *fallen* (*Verfallen*) into the world it is concerned with and into the public interpretation of the "one." Falling shows itself as idle talk, which passes on what is said without appropriating it; curiosity, a restless seeing for the sake of seeing, which Heidegger traces to [[Augustine of Hippo|Augustine]]'s "lust of the eyes" (SZ 171); and ambiguity, in which it is no longer possible to tell what was genuinely understood (SZ §§35–37).

## Anxiety, care, and truth

What could bring Dasein back from its absorption? Heidegger's answer is a mood. Fear is always fear of something definite within the world that threatens. Anxiety (*Angst*) has no such object.

> Das Wovor der Angst ist das In-der-Welt-sein als solches.
>
> What anxiety is anxious about is being-in-the-world as such.
>
> (SZ 186)

In anxiety what threatens is "nothing and nowhere" (SZ 186). The world of equipment and the public interpretation of the "one" lose their significance, and Dasein feels *uncanny* (*unheimlich*), literally not at home (SZ 188–189). Anxiety thereby individualizes Dasein and confronts it with its own being as a possibility for which it alone is responsible. In a footnote Heidegger credits [[Søren Kierkegaard|Kierkegaard]] with having gone furthest in the analysis of anxiety (SZ 190).

The structural whole that anxiety discloses is *care* (*Sorge*).

> Das Sein des Daseins besagt: Sich-vorweg-schon-sein-in-(der-Welt-) als Sein-bei (innerweltlich begegnendem Seienden).
>
> The being of Dasein means: ahead-of-itself-already-being-in-(the-world) as being-alongside (beings encountered within the world).
>
> (SZ 192)

The formula has three moments. *Ahead of itself* names existence, Dasein's being toward its possibilities. *Already in a world* names facticity, its thrownness. *Alongside beings* names falling, its absorption in what it is concerned with. Care here is not worry or solicitude but the title for this whole structure. Heidegger confirms it with a Latin fable from Hyginus, in which Cura, Care, shapes the first human being from clay and is granted possession of it for as long as it lives (SZ 197–198).

The analysis of world has a consequence for truth (SZ §44). The traditional definition, truth as the agreement of intellect and thing (*adaequatio intellectus et rei*, in [[Thomas Aquinas|Aquinas]]'s formula; SZ 214), presupposes something more basic. An assertion is true when it uncovers the being it is about as it is in itself, and it can do so only because Dasein is already in a world where beings can be uncovered. Heidegger connects this with the Greek word for truth, *aletheia*, which he reads as un-concealment (SZ 219).

> Es gibt Wahrheit nur, sofern und solange Dasein ist.
>
> There is truth only insofar as and as long as Dasein is.
>
> (SZ 226)

Heidegger draws the consequence himself: before Newton, Newton's laws were neither true nor false, and the same holds for the principle of non-contradiction and for every truth whatsoever (SZ 226–227). He adds at once that this does not make truth subjective. The beings Newton's laws uncover were there before Newton. What did not exist before Dasein is their uncoveredness.

## Being-toward-death

Care seems to make an analysis of the *whole* of Dasein impossible. As long as Dasein exists, it is ahead of itself, and something is still outstanding; when nothing is outstanding any more, Dasein no longer exists (SZ §46). Death is the end that would make Dasein whole, but it cannot be experienced by the one who dies, and the deaths of others are not a substitute, since no one can take another's dying away from them (SZ 240). Death must therefore be understood not as an event at the end of life but as a way of being toward that end. Heidegger quotes *Der Ackermann aus Böhmen*, a dialogue written around 1400:

> Sobald ein Mensch zum Leben kommt, sogleich ist er alt genug zu sterben.
>
> As soon as a human being comes to life, he is at once old enough to die.
>
> (SZ 245)

Everyday Dasein evades this. "One dies," it says (SZ 253), as if death were a public event that concerns everyone and so no one in particular. Against this evasion Heidegger gives a full existential concept of death.

> Der Tod als Ende des Daseins ist die eigenste, unbezügliche, gewisse und als solche unbestimmte, unüberholbare Möglichkeit des Daseins.
>
> As the end of Dasein, death is its most proper possibility, one that relates it to no one else, that is certain and, as certain, indeterminate, and that cannot be overtaken.
>
> (SZ 258–259)

The definition has five parts.

- **Ownmost** (*eigenste*). In death Dasein's own being is at stake, and no one can die for another.
- **Non-relational** (*unbezügliche*). Death cuts all relations to others and to the world of concern; in it Dasein stands alone.
- **Certain** (*gewisse*). Its certainty is not the empirical certainty of a statistic, which would be only highly probable, but the certainty of a possibility Dasein already is.
- **Indefinite** (*unbestimmte*). It is possible at any moment, and when it will come is undetermined.
- **Not to be outstripped** (*unüberholbare*). It is the possibility beyond which there are no further possibilities.

[[Epicurus]] argued that death is nothing to us, since where death is, we are not. Heidegger agrees that death is never an experience, and draws the opposite conclusion: death matters not as something that will happen to me but as a possibility I am now. To *anticipate* (*vorlaufen*) death is not to brood on it but to understand oneself in the light of it. This frees Dasein from the "one" and lets it grasp its other possibilities as its own, which Heidegger calls "freedom toward death" (SZ 266).

Attestation that this is a real possibility for Dasein comes from conscience. The call of conscience says nothing and reports no facts; it speaks only in the mode of silence (SZ 273), summoning Dasein from its lostness in the "one" to its ownmost potentiality for being. Heeding it is *resoluteness* (*Entschlossenheit*), and its authentic form is *anticipatory resoluteness* (SZ §62). Heidegger refuses to say what one should resolve upon: only the resolution itself can answer that (SZ 298). Critics from Löwith onward have called this decisionism, a readiness to decide that is indifferent to what is decided, and some have seen in it a point of entry for his politics.

## Temporality

What makes the whole of care possible?

> Die Zeitlichkeit enthüllt sich als der Sinn der eigentlichen Sorge.
>
> Temporality shows itself to be the meaning of authentic care.
>
> (SZ 326)

Each moment of care is rooted in one of three *ecstases* of temporality, so called because each is a way of standing outside oneself (Greek *ekstasis*). Temporality is not something that is and then also moves; it is the original "outside itself" (SZ 329).

| Moment of care | Ecstasis of temporality |
| --- | --- |
| Ahead of itself (existence) | Future (*Zukunft*): coming toward oneself |
| Already in a world (facticity) | Having-been (*Gewesenheit*): coming back to what one already is |
| Alongside beings (falling) | Present (*Gegenwart*): letting beings be encountered |

The order matters. The primary phenomenon of original and authentic temporality, Heidegger writes, is the future (SZ 329). Dasein comes back to what it has been only by coming toward itself from its possibilities, and it lets beings be present only from out of that unity. From temporality Heidegger then derives the *historicity* of Dasein (SZ §§72–77) and, last, the ordinary concept of time as an endless sequence of nows (SZ §81). That concept runs from [[Aristotle]]'s definition of time as the number of motion with respect to before and after (*Physics* IV 11, 219b1–2) to [[Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel|Hegel]] (SZ §82). For Heidegger, it is derivative: it levels down the time of concern, in which there is always time *for* something, into a neutral series of points.

*Being and Time* breaks off here. Its two published divisions were to be followed by a third, "Time and Being," showing time as the horizon for understanding being as such, and by a second part carrying out the destruction of the history of ontology through Kant, Descartes, and Aristotle. Neither appeared. The book ends with questions:

> Führt ein Weg von der ursprünglichen Zeit zum Sinn des Seins? Offenbart sich die Zeit selbst als Horizont des Seins?
>
> Is there a path that leads from original time to the meaning of being? Does time itself reveal itself as the horizon of being?
>
> (SZ 437)

The lecture course *Basic Problems of Phenomenology* (1927, GA 24), which Heidegger later described as a new elaboration of the missing third division, comes closest to an answer.

## Kant, Davos, and the nothing

In *Kant and the Problem of Metaphysics* (1929) Heidegger read the *Critique of Pure Reason* not as a theory of knowledge but as a laying of the foundations of metaphysics, centered on the finitude of human knowing. He argued that the transcendental imagination is the hidden common root of sensibility and understanding, and that Kant, having glimpsed this unknown root, shrank back from it in the second edition (KPM §31). In March 1929 he defended this reading at Davos in a public debate with Ernst Cassirer, the leading Neo-Kantian. Rudolf Carnap was in the audience.

In July of the same year Heidegger gave his inaugural lecture at Freiburg, "What Is Metaphysics?" The sciences, he observed, investigate beings and nothing besides. But what about this nothing? Logic answers that the nothing is the negation of the totality of beings, which presupposes the "not" of negation. Heidegger reverses the order: the nothing is more original than the "not" and negation, and it is disclosed not by an act of thought but by anxiety, in which beings as a whole slip away.

> Das Nichts selbst nichtet.
>
> The nothing itself nothings.
>
> (WM)

Heidegger knew how this would sound. Within the lecture he grants that, by the rules of logic, both the question and any answer about the nothing are self-contradictory, and he concludes that logic is not the court before which the question can be tried: the very idea of "logic" dissolves in the whirl of a more original questioning (WM). The lecture ends with the question that [[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz|Leibniz]] had called the first one we have a right to ask, and that opens *Introduction to Metaphysics* (EM) six years later: Why are there beings at all, and not rather nothing?

### Remark: Carnap and the logic of "nothing"

Carnap took up the challenge in "The Overcoming of Metaphysics through the Logical Analysis of Language" (1932), where Heidegger's lecture is the main example. His argument can be laid out as a table of three columns, condensed here into my own notation.

| | I. Meaningful sentences | II. From sense to nonsense | III. Logically correct language |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | "What is outside?" "Outside there is rain." $\mathrm{Out}(r)$ | "What is outside?" "Outside there is nothing." $\mathrm{Out}(\mathit{no})$ | "There is nothing that is outside." $\neg\exists x\, \mathrm{Out}(x)$ |
| B | "We know the rain." "The rain rains." | "We know the nothing." "The nothing nothings." | None of these forms can be built. |

In column II, "nothing" occupies the grammatical position of the name "rain," and that is the whole mistake. In first-order logic, "nothing is outside" is not a predication about an object called nothing. It is a quantified sentence, $\neg\exists x\, \mathrm{Out}(x)$, equivalently $\forall x\, \neg\mathrm{Out}(x)$, and there is no term in it for the questions of row B to be about. That no term can do the job is easy to prove.

**Proposition.** Let $L$ be a first-order language with a unary predicate symbol $P$, and let $t$ be a closed term of $L$. If $\mathcal{M}$ is an $L$-structure with $P^{\mathcal{M}} = \varnothing$, then
$$
\mathcal{M} \models \neg\exists x\, P(x) \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathcal{M} \not\models P(t).
$$
In particular, no closed term $t$ makes $P(t) \leftrightarrow \neg\exists x\, P(x)$ valid.

**Proof.** Since $P^{\mathcal{M}}$ is empty, no element of the domain satisfies $P$, so $\mathcal{M} \models \neg\exists x\, P(x)$. But $\mathcal{M} \models P(t)$ would require $t^{\mathcal{M}} \in P^{\mathcal{M}} = \varnothing$, which is impossible. Such structures exist, since any nonempty domain with $P$ interpreted as $\varnothing$ will do, so the biconditional is not valid. $\blacksquare$

In words: "nothing is outside" is true exactly in the situations where whatever "nothing" might name is *not* outside, so the word cannot be read as a name. Carnap adds that even if one were allowed to introduce "nothing" as the name of an object, its definition would deny that object existence, and Heidegger's sentences would contradict themselves. The verb "to nothing" is simply a new word without meaning. The analysis continues a line this series has already followed: [[Immanuel Kant|Kant]]'s claim that being is not a real predicate, and [[Gottlob Frege|Frege]]'s treatment of existence as a property of concepts. %% link: quantifiers and structures in Logic %%

Heidegger's position is not refuted by this proposition so much as rejected at its premise. He did not overlook the logical form of negation; he denied that logic is the final authority on what can be asked. In the 1943 postscript to the lecture he insisted that the nothing is not an object at all but the other of all beings, which he called the veil of being. Michael Friedman's *A Parting of the Ways* (2000) reads Davos and Carnap's essay together as the moment at which [[Vienna Circle#Carnap|Carnap]]'s analytic philosophy and Heidegger's continental philosophy went their separate ways.

## The turn

Heidegger later explained why the third division of *Being and Time* was never published.

> Der fragliche Abschnitt wurde zurückgehalten, weil das Denken im zureichenden Sagen dieser Kehre versagte und mit Hilfe der Sprache der Metaphysik nicht durchkam.
>
> The division was withheld because thinking could not find adequate words for this turn and did not get through with the language of metaphysics.
>
> (BH)

The *turn* (*Kehre*) reverses the direction of inquiry. *Being and Time* moved from Dasein's understanding of being toward being. The later work asks how being gives itself to human beings, differently in different epochs, and Heidegger insisted that the turn lay in the matter itself, not merely in a change of his standpoint.

The result is a *history of being*. For the early Greek thinkers, [[Milesian School#Anaximander|Anaximander]], [[Heraclitus]], and [[Eleatic School#Parmenides|Parmenides]], being was experienced as *physis*, emerging and self-showing, and truth as *aletheia*, unconcealment. With [[Plato]], being becomes the *idea*, the look of a thing as it is seen, and truth becomes the correctness of the seeing ("Plato's Doctrine of Truth," 1942). From then on metaphysics thinks being only as the beingness of beings and grounds it in a highest being: it is, in Heidegger's later term, *onto-theology*. The concept passes through *energeia*, the created being of medieval theology, and the representing subject of [[René Descartes|Descartes]], to Nietzsche's will to power, which Heidegger reads as a will to will and as the completion of metaphysics. Being itself, as distinct from beings, has been forgotten all along, and this *forgetting of being* is not a mistake of particular thinkers but the way being has withdrawn.

In the 1930s Heidegger began to write *Seyn*, an archaic spelling, to mark being as distinct from the being of metaphysics, and in 1955 he wrote the word crossed out. His private *Contributions to Philosophy* (written 1936–1938, published 1989) think being as *Ereignis*, the "event" in which being and human beings come into their own. The late essays move away from argument toward close readings of the early Greeks and of Hölderlin, and toward a style that Heidegger himself preferred to call "thinking" rather than philosophy.

## The work of art

"The Origin of the Work of Art" (UK) treats the artwork neither as a thing with aesthetic properties nor as the expression of an artist's experience, but as a place where truth happens. Heidegger's examples are a painting by Van Gogh of a pair of shoes, which he reads as opening up the world of a peasant woman's labor, and a Greek temple, which, standing on its rock, first gathers around itself the world of a people: birth and death, victory and disgrace. In the work, *world*, the open context of meaning in which a people lives, and *earth*, what shelters itself and resists being made fully explicit, such as the stone of the temple or the color of the painting, are held in a strife that the work keeps open. The essence of art is then the truth of beings putting itself into the work (UK). Meyer Schapiro objected in 1968 that the shoes were Van Gogh's own, and [[Jacques Derrida|Derrida]] devoted a long essay to the dispute.

## Technology

> Demgemäß ist auch das Wesen der Technik ganz und gar nichts Technisches.
>
> Accordingly, the essence of technology is nothing technological at all.
>
> (FT)

Modern technology, Heidegger argues, is not a set of instruments but a way in which beings are revealed. Older technology, the windmill or the peasant's plough, worked with nature; modern technology *challenges* nature to deliver energy that can be extracted, stored, and switched about. Everything is thereby revealed as *standing reserve* (*Bestand*), resources on call for further ordering. Heidegger's example is the Rhine, which appears as a supplier of water pressure once a power plant is built into it, and which is no longer the river of Hölderlin's hymn "The Rhine." The essence of technology is *enframing* (*Gestell*), the gathering of this challenging into a single way of revealing. The danger is not machines but that enframing becomes the only way in which anything can show up at all, human beings included, as the "supply of patients" of a clinic shows. Heidegger closes with lines of Hölderlin:

> Wo aber Gefahr ist, wächst / Das Rettende auch.
>
> But where danger is, there grows / what saves as well.
>
> (Hölderlin, "Patmos")

In a 1966 interview with *Der Spiegel*, published after his death, Heidegger said that philosophy had been replaced by cybernetics and that only a god could still save us.

## Language and dwelling

The *Letter on Humanism* (1947), written in reply to a question from the French philosopher Jean Beaufret, is Heidegger's clearest statement of the late position.

> Die Sprache ist das Haus des Seins. In ihrer Behausung wohnt der Mensch.
>
> Language is the house of being. In its dwelling, human beings live.
>
> (BH)

Human beings do not possess language as a tool; they live in it, and through it beings come to show themselves. The *Letter* also settles accounts with [[Jean-Paul Sartre|Sartre]]. Sartre had declared that existence precedes essence and that existentialism is a humanism. Heidegger replies that reversing a metaphysical proposition still leaves a metaphysical proposition (BH), and that every humanism either rests on a metaphysics or makes itself the ground of one. His own formula puts the human being in a different place: not the lord of beings but the shepherd of being (BH). The late essays develop this as an ethics of *dwelling*, a releasement (*Gelassenheit*) toward things that lets them be, and a gathering of earth and sky, divinities and mortals, that Heidegger calls the *fourfold*.

## After Heidegger

- [[Jean-Paul Sartre|Sartre]]'s *Being and Nothingness* (1943) recast the existential analytic as a philosophy of consciousness and freedom, a reading Heidegger rejected in the *Letter on Humanism*.
- [[Emmanuel Levinas|Levinas]], who attended Heidegger's lectures in Freiburg in 1928–29, came to argue that ontology itself is a philosophy of power and that ethics, the relation to the other person, is first philosophy (*Totality and Infinity*, 1961).
- [[Maurice Merleau-Ponty|Merleau-Ponty]]'s *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945) gave being-in-the-world a body, which *Being and Time* conspicuously lacks.
- Hans-Georg Gadamer developed Heidegger's hermeneutic circle into a philosophical hermeneutics (*Truth and Method*, 1960), and Hannah Arendt's *The Human Condition* (1958) carried his questions into political theory.
- [[Jacques Derrida|Derrida]]'s deconstruction takes its name from Heidegger's *Destruktion*, and [[Michel Foucault|Foucault]] said in his last interview that Heidegger had always been for him the essential philosopher.
- In analytic philosophy, Gilbert Ryle reviewed *Being and Time* in *Mind* (1929) with admiration and serious doubts, and Carnap made Heidegger his example of metaphysical nonsense. Hubert Dreyfus later argued (*What Computers Can't Do*, 1972) that symbolic AI failed because it tried to represent as explicit facts the background of practical familiarity that Heidegger described as readiness-to-hand, an argument that influenced Terry Winograd and Fernando Flores's *Understanding Computers and Cognition* (1986).
- Oskar Becker's *Mathematische Existenz*, published in the same 1927 volume of Husserl's yearbook as *Being and Time*, read [[Philosophy of Mathematics#Intuitionism|Brouwer's intuitionism]] in the light of the existential analytic.
- The political debate has a history of its own, from Jürgen Habermas's 1953 review of *Introduction to Metaphysics* through Victor Farías's *Heidegger and Nazism* (1987) to the *Black Notebooks*.

## Reading order

In *Being and Time*, the Introduction (§§1–8), the analysis of worldhood (§§14–18), the "one" (§§25–27), anxiety and care (§§39–41), death (§§46–53), and temporality (§§61–66) give the line of the argument. The lecture course *History of the Concept of Time* (1925, GA 20) is an earlier and often clearer draft of the first division, and *Basic Problems of Phenomenology* (1927, GA 24) continues where the book stops. After that, "What Is Metaphysics?" and the *Letter on Humanism* show the transition, and "The Origin of the Work of Art" and "The Question Concerning Technology" give the late work at its most accessible.
